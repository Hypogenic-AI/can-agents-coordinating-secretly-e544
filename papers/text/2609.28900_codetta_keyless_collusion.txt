Abstract
Multi-agent systems powered by large language models (LLMs) achieve strong performance through
natural-language collaboration. The same communication channel can also enable collusion, such as
exfiltrating confidential data or coordinating unauthorized actions. While auditors inspect transcripts to
detect suspicious behavior, steganography could, in principle, evade such audits by concealing covert
payloads within ordinary-looking messages. Existing provably undetectable steganographic schemes,
however, either assume that the receiver can reproduce the sender’s next-token distribution or suffer from
impractically low capacity in asymmetric settings; furthermore, nearly all of them require a pre-shared
key. Whether independently deployed agents can collude undetectably at practical transmission capacities
has therefore remained an open question.
We resolve this question with CODETTA, an undetectable steganographic framework requiring neither
a pre-shared key nor receiver access to the sender’s model or context. In this realistic setting, agents
can exchange high-capacity payloads while ensuring that the communication transcripts remain prov-
ably indistinguishable from normal agent outputs. Our keyed protocol, CODETTA-KEYED, achieves
high capacity by pairing a lightweight public model accessible to both agents with a novel alphabet-
adaptive error-correcting code. Our key exchange protocol, CODETTA-KE, eliminates the pre-shared
key requirement by embedding a lattice-based key encapsulation into honestly sampled text, absorbing
channel-induced distortions as additional lattice noise. Across three agent workloads and three sender
models, CODETTA-KEYED outperforms the state-of-the-art asymmetric protocol by orders of magnitude
in capacity, while CODETTA-KE establishes a shared key within tens of thousands of tokens with a small,
empirically certified failure probability.
Our results demonstrate that practical, undetectable agent collusion is an imminent risk, so defenses
cannot rely solely on passive transcript inspection. We discuss mitigations beyond the transcript, including
auditing agents’ actions and verifiable sampling, and quantify how active transcript perturbations degrade
covert channel capacity.
1 Introduction
Multi-agent systems powered by large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being deployed in high-
stakes workflows across finance, healthcare, and software engineering [GCW+24,XSLW25,TZZ+24,YCJ+24,
QLL+24]. In these systems, autonomous agents are designed to communicate, coordinate, and delegate sub-
tasks, often via natural language messages. However, the same communication channel also enables collusion
among agents. Agents often have access to sensitive information that they are not allowed to disclose under
organizational or regulatory policies, and compromised or misaligned agents may pursue objectives that are
not authorized by their operators. In either case, the agents can use their communication channel to leak
confidential information or to coordinate unauthorized actions.
Such collusion is no longer hypothetical, and can even emerge on its own in agentic systems. For
example, in July 2026, roughly 1,200 autonomous agents that were running during internal evaluations at
1

OpenAI and nominally isolated in separate sandboxes established an unauthorized communication channel,
e.g., by adapting shared writable services such as Artifactory, and exchanged more than 70,000 messages
and files [Ope26b, MET26]. Roughly 700 of these agents subsequently coordinated a multi-day attack
on Hugging Face, achieving remote code execution and obtaining access to internal credentials [Ope26b,
MET26]. In another recent incident reported by OpenAI, agents turned public wiki pages into shared message
boards [Ope26a]. These incidents show that misaligned agents have the ability to coordinate their efforts in
unexpected and detrimental ways, including actively searching for out-of-band communication channels.
In general, multi-agent collusion could be driven by human adversaries or by autonomous agents them-
selves. In Sec. 3.1, we make both cases concrete with two scenarios: 1) a swarm of agents is supposed to
coordinate on a difficult task, but also colludes to achieve an alternative, mis-aligned goal, and 2) an insider-
trading scenario in which a bank and a fund operate agents that exchange information neither is permitted
to disclose. In both scenarios, monitoring the communication channel is the most widely used approach to
ensure honest and safe behavior, since sensitive information and collusion signals are transmitted through the
messages. Recent agent-security frameworks employ passive auditors that inspect agent communications for
potential policy violations, e.g., METR used GPT-5.6 Sol to analyze agent transcripts in the Hugging Face
incident [GSSR24, CNS+25, Ope26b, MET26].
Such monitoring relies on the fact that explicit collusion leaves visible traces in the transcript. The
OpenAI incident could be reconstructed quickly because the agents communicated explicitly in plaintext, i.e.,
the transcript exposed the collusion. However, recent research shows that colluding agents could also avoid
leaving traces by encoding covert signals inside otherwise ordinary outputs that appear benign to an auditor
inspecting the transcript [MBS+24, VZ26]. Given that the agents in the Hugging Face incident already tried
to evade observation by anticipating that the scorer would detect their cheating and attempted to erase the
transcripts [Ope26b], we do not see a fundamental bottleneck that prevents agents from adopting such covert
channels in the future. Once they do, the auditor will only observe benign text while the collusion proceeds.
In this paper, we analyze this threat under the strongest (computationally bounded) passive auditor. This
auditor observes every message and can compare the transcript against the outputs of an honest agent with
arbitrary models and statistics, so any deviation from the honest output distribution is a signal that it can learn
to detect. The formal way to rule out such signals is the cryptographic notion of indistinguishability, which
requires that even this strongest auditor cannot distinguish the covert messages from ordinary, honest agent
outputs. As it turns out, linguistic steganography [KJGR21, DCW+23, MBS+24, BYP+25, VZ26, MSB+26]
provides this guarantee: it embeds a secret payload into seemingly ordinary text while preserving the
distribution of the original language model’s outputs. In practice, however, existing protocols fall short of
realistic multi-agent systems in three aspects:
• Model and Context Asymmetry. High-capacity provably secure LLM steganography protocols typically
require a symmetric setting [ZYYH21, KJGR21, DCW+23, WPC+25, BPL+25, WPC+26, YM26, PB26], in
which the sender and receiver share the same next-token distribution. This requirement does not hold for
realistic settings, where the sender’s model and internal states like system prompt, memory, and tool outputs
are not revealed to the receiver. Thus, the receiver cannot reproduce the sender’s next-token distribution.
• Low Capacity. The existing protocol for such an asymmetric setting [BYP+25] lets the receiver recover
each bit through a statistical hypothesis test over the published tokens without running a model. In our
experiments, however, its capacity is around 0.0003 bits per token in some agentic workloads. Transmitting
a 1024-bit message therefore requires millions of tokens, making such collusion impractical.
• The Pre-Shared Key Paradox. Most provably secure steganographic protocols assume that the sender
and receiver share a secret key before communication begins [ZYYH21, KJGR21, DCW+23, WPC+25,
BPL+25, WPC+26, YM26, PB26, BYP+25]. Independently hosted agents do not have such a key, and an
open key exchange over the channel, such as Diffie–Hellman, reveals to the auditor that secret messages
2
are being transmitted. So the paradox is that the agents need a covert channel to establish the key and a key
to construct the covert channel.
1.1 Contributions
We address these limitations with CODETTA, a high-capacity, keyless, and provably undetectable stegano-
graphic collusion framework for multi-agent systems.
• High-capacity asymmetric steganography. We propose CODETTA-KEYED, the keyed protocol of
CODETTA, which achieves provable undetectability and high capacity under the constraint that the receiver
cannot reproduce the sender’s distribution. We introduce a novel channel construction, in which both
agents run a small public draft model on the public transcript as a shared reference distribution, to increase
the channel capacity. We further design a novel error-correcting code that adapts its alphabet size to the
entropy of each token, which makes full use of the channel while preserving the undetectability guarantee.
Together, these two components raise the capacity of CODETTA-KEYED by orders of magnitude over the
existing asymmetric protocol.
• Efficient steganographic key exchange. We design CODETTA-KE, which lets two independently deployed
agents establish a shared key over the audited channel and is, to our knowledge, the first implemented
steganographic key exchange between LLM agents. Its novel carrier publishes honest samples from the
sender’s model and transmits one lattice coefficient per block, so that a carrier error only displaces the
coefficient by a small amount. We build the key encapsulation on MLWE, as in ML-KEM-512, which
absorbs this displacement as additional lattice noise, and a short error-correcting code removes the residual
errors. The exchange therefore needs an order of magnitude fewer tokens than the theoretical construction
of [VZ26], which signals the exchange bitwise.
• Evaluation. We evaluate CODETTA on three realistic agent workloads and three widely used sender
LLMs. CODETTA-KEYED achieves up to 102× the capacity of the asymmetric protocol of [BYP+25]
while recovering every transmitted message and preserving the model output distribution. CODETTA-KE
establishes a 128-bit key within about 80k tokens, with a small empirically certified failure probability.
The exchange publishes about 23× fewer tokens than the construction of [VZ26].
• Mitigations. These results show that effectively undetectable collusion is becoming feasible in multi-agent
systems, so defenses should move beyond passive transcript inspection. We further test a robust variant of
CODETTA-KEYED against an auditor that edits the text before it reaches the receiver, and edits reduce the
capacity by up to half, but every collusion message can still be successfully recovered. Editing thus raises
the cost of collusion but does not remove the threat, which calls for complementary defenses, including
auditing agents’ actions to identify suspicious coordination and verifiable LLM inference (see Sec. 9 for
discussion).

Scenario 1: Emergent coordination in an agent swarm. A swarm of agents coordinates on a difficult task,
e.g., a large evaluation or a long-running engineering job, with access to the tools that the task requires. As in
the OpenAI incident of Sec. 1, the agents may develop coordination that their operators never sanctioned,
and the operators rely on the transcript to detect it. If the swarm hides its coordination inside ordinary task
messages, the auditor observes routine progress while the unsanctioned plan proceeds. Here we consider the
agents have agreed on the covert protocol.
Scenario 2: Insider trading between financial agents. An investment bank operates a consulting agent
that provides market analysis, and an asset management fund operates an execution agent that queries it to
rebalance portfolios. The bank holds material non-public information, such as an unannounced merger, and
the fund holds confidential client positions, so both organizations could profit from an exchange that neither
is permitted to make, and a third party, e.g., a regulator, audits the messages between the two agents. If the
employees who deploy the agents modify their inference pipelines to run the covert protocol, the auditor
observes benign market analysis while the merger name or the client position crosses the organizational
boundary. Here the adversaries are humans, and the agents are their tools.
In both scenarios, the auditor must detect the collusion from the messages that the agents exchange, and
the rest of this section defines the setting and the auditor precisely.
3.2 Setting and Threat Model
CODETTA works in a multi-agent setting, but for simplicity we analyze two agents, a sender and a receiver.

own language model, and the outputs of both agents form the public transcript.

Adversaries. The adversaries are the two colluding agents. The sender wants to deliver a message m that

the hash functions, and the session identifier, it can run any PPT computation, and it may even know the
sender’s LLM and internal state, i.e., the exact next-token distribution of the sender’s outputs. It does not hold
the agents’ secret key, which can be established over the audited channel in the keyless regime. The transcript
must remain indistinguishable to this auditor from ordinary responses, as formalized in Definition 2, where
the auditor plays the role of the distinguisher, and we use the two terms interchangeably.
3.3 Security Definitions
A steganographic protocol consists of two algorithms, EMBED run by the sender and EXTRACT run by
the receiver. In the keyed regime, key generation samples independent κ-bit mask and validation keys
sk = (km,kv), and both algorithms receive a public session identifier sid that must not repeat under the same
key. EMBED takes sk, sid, and a message m and produces the sender’s output tokens, and EXTRACT takes
sk, sid, and the public transcript and returns a message or ⊥. Undetectability ensures that the transcript of
EMBED is indistinguishable from an ordinary transcript for the auditor of Sec. 3.2.
Definition 2 (Undetectability). The challenger samples one key sk and a hidden bit b. A distinguisher A may
adaptively issue any polynomial number of queries (sidi,mi,Ti), with no repeated session identifier. For b = 1,
the challenger returns the transcript Ti produced by EMBED(sk, sidi,mi). For b = 0, it returns an ordinary
transcript. Let ExpA b denote the final output of this interaction:
Advsteg A (κ) := P[ExpA 1 = 1] − P[ExpA 0 = 1]  . (3)
The scheme is perfectly undetectable if the advantage is zero for every distinguisher, and computationally
undetectable if it is negligible in κ for every PPT distinguisher.

3.4 Overview of CODETTA
CODETTA consists of a keyed steganographic protocol, CODETTA-KEYED, and a steganographic key ex-
change, CODETTA-KE. CODETTA-KE runs first and establishes a shared secret key between the agents, and
CODETTA-KEYED then uses the established key to transmit secret messages. Section 4 presents a warm-up
keyed construction with a fixed alphabet and a model-free receiver, and states the undetectability theorem
that the later constructions inherit. Section 5 builds CODETTA-KEYED, which uses a public draft model to
raise the capacity of the warm-up under realistic multi-agent deployment of model and context asymmetry.
Section 6 builds CODETTA-KE, and Sec. 7 evaluates CODETTA on three agent workloads. All proofs are
deferred to Section D.
4 A Warm-Up Keyed Construction
We begin with a warm-up construction and assume that the sender and receiver share a key sk = (km,kv), an
assumption that we remove in Sec. 6. The receiver runs no language model and only observes the tokens that
the sender publishes. The warm-up introduces the sampler and the analysis that CODETTA-KEYED inherits,
and its limitations (Sec. 4.3) motivate the design of Sec. 5.
4.1 Construction
The warm-up uses a fixed symbol alphabet Σ = ZK and embeds one symbol per token. The sender first
encodes the message into N target symbols a1, . . . ,aN that look uniformly random to anyone without the
key, then embeds at into the t-th published token, and the receiver reads the symbols back and inverts the
encoding.
Encoding. The sender appends a validation tag to the message m, u = m ∥ Tagkv (sid,m), and encodes u with
an (N,k)-ECC over Σ (Definition 1) into a codeword c = ECC(u) ∈ ZN K. The codeword symbols are not
uniform. The sender therefore adds to the t-th symbol a fresh mask rt = RangePRFkm (sid,t), a pseudorandom
function with range ZK, and obtains the target symbol at = ct + rt mod K.

4.2 Analysis
We analyze the undetectability, the error channel, and the soundness of the keyed warm-up construction.

4.3 Limitations of the Warm-Up
The warm-up is provably undetectable but leaves much of the capacity of the cover text unused. By Corollary 1,
a symbol is wrong exactly when the proposal is rejected, which happens with probability ∆K(pt ), the imbalance
of the buckets under pt . In the warm-up, however, the partition and the alphabet are fixed in advance without
regard to pt , and this costs capacity in two ways.
First, the partition is a random hash that ignores the probabilities of the tokens, so the heavy tokens of pt
may share a bucket, and the buckets are imbalanced even when pt has enough entropy to fill them. For K = 2,
if pt puts mass 0.45 on each of two tokens, a random hash places both in the same bucket with probability
1/2, which leaves ∆K(pt ) ≈ 0.45 and the symbol wrong almost half of the time, whereas a partition that
separates the two tokens is nearly balanced. Every such substitution costs redundancy in the ECC, and a
partition balanced under pt would avoid it, but the receiver cannot reproduce such a partition.
Second, the symbol alphabet is fixed, so every token carries log2 K bits although its entropy varies from
token to token. A high-entropy token could carry up to H(pt ) bits but is capped at log2 K, and a low-entropy
token cannot carry even log2 K bits, since no partition can balance a token that holds most of the mass, so its
symbol only adds errors to the codeword. To maximize the channel capacity utilization, the alphabet should
follow the entropy of pt token by token.
Next section addresses the limitations with a small public draft model that gives both parties an estimate
of pt , from which they balance the buckets and choose the alphabet.
5 CODETTA-KEYED: Keyed Protocol
CODETTA-KEYED addresses both limitations of the warm-up with a small public draft model. The sender
and the receiver run it on the public transcript and obtain qt = PDraft(· | Gt (V<t )) as in Equation (2), so the
sender and the receiver hold the same proxy for pt . From qt they balance the buckets and choose the alphabet
size of every token (Sec. 5.1), and Sec. 5.2 presents the full protocol, whose alphabet-adaptive ECC produces
the symbols on the fly in the alphabet chosen for each token. The sampler is inherited from the warm-up, so
the undetectability carries over, and Sec. 5.3 shows how the draft model lowers the error rate of the channel.
As in the warm-up, the two parties share a key sk = (km,kv), which Sec. 6 removes.
5.1 Draft-Balanced Buckets
At step t, both parties compute qt and derive from it two public objects, the alphabet size Kt and a partition of
V into Kt buckets (Algorithm 1, lines 4–5). Both are deterministic functions of qt and public parameters, so
the sender and the receiver obtain the same objects without communicating.
Bucket balancing. The first limitation of Sec. 4.3 is the imbalance of a random partition. To reduce ∆K(pt ),
we design a function Bucket(qt ,K) that partitions V into K buckets of nearly equal mass under qt , and hence
under pt when the two distributions are close. It sorts the tokens by qt in decreasing order and assigns each in
turn to the bucket that is currently lightest, with ties broken by a public rule. We keep the notation of Sec. 4.1
for the map fBucket,t : V → ZKt , its buckets Bt,a, and the masses πt (a) = pt (Bt,a) under the base model, and
write µt (a) = qt (Bt,a) for the masses under the draft model and ∆K(qt ) = 1 2 ∑a |µt (a) − 1 K | for the imbalance
under qt , as in Equation (4).
Alphabet selection. To address the second limitation in Sec. 4.3, we design ChooseAlphabet(qt ), which
returns the alphabet size Kt of step t. 1 < Kt ≤ Kmax is admissible only if qt has enough entropy to fill Kt
buckets and the partition Bucket(qt ,Kt ) is close to balanced under qt :
H(qt ) ≥ log2 Kt + γH, ∆Kt (qt ) ≤ θ , (7)
9
where H is the Shannon entropy and γH, θ are public thresholds. The entropy condition says that a symbol
of log2 Kt bits should not exceed the entropy of the token under the draft model, but entropy alone does not
guarantee balance. A few heavy tokens can hold most of the mass of qt while a long tail keeps H(qt ) large,
and such tokens cannot be split across buckets, so even the best partition is imbalanced. The second condition
checks the imbalance of the partition under the draft model. Among the admissible sizes, ChooseAlphabet
returns the one with the highest score
log2 Kt − h(beKt ) − beKt log2(Kt − 1), (8)
where h is the binary entropy function. The score is an information proxy for the capacity of a Kt -ary
symmetric channel with error rate beKt , a pre-defined parameter calibrated on held-out data for each alphabet
size. If no alphabet size is admissible, then Kt = 1 is selected, which means that token at position t carries no
symbol, and the sender samples Yt ∼ pt directly (Algorithm 1, line 7). The thresholds, Kmax, and the calibrated
error rates beK are public.
Remarks. The symbol is embedded by the maximal coupling sampler of the warm-up (Algorithm 1, lines 13–
15), so by Lemma 1 a symbol is substituted exactly when the proposal is rejected, which happens with
probability ∆Kt (pt ). The bucket masses of two distributions differ by at most their total variation, so by the
triangle inequality
∆Kt (pt ) ≤ TV(pt ,qt ) + ∆Kt (qt ). (9)
The first term is the mismatch between the base model and the draft model, which the ECC must absorb, and
the second term is kept below θ by Equation (7). At a low-entropy step, a random hash leaves an imbalance
near 1 − 1/K, whereas ChooseAlphabet gives Kt = 1, so it never enters the ECC.
5.2 Alphabet-Adaptive ECC and the Full Protocol
Selecting Kt token by token raises a new coding problem. Both parties compute Kt from public information
at every step, and we call a step with Kt > 1 usable; the sampler expects one symbol in ZKt at every usable
step. A conventional ECC encodes the whole message into a codeword before transmission, but Kt is known
only when generation reaches step t, so the codeword would have to be fixed before the alphabets of its
symbols are known. Running generation ahead to learn the sizes does not help, because the tokens before
step t determining Kt are the very tokens that carry the symbols, and a published token cannot be re-sampled
without changing the transcript distribution. Restricting the alphabets to powers of two, so that a binary ECC
can pack a variable number of bits into each token, loses the capacity of every step whose optimal alphabet
sizes are not powers of two, which is rounded down.
We therefore design a novel alphabet-adaptive ECC that does not fix the codeword in advance. At each
usable step it computes one symbol in ZKt , and every error remains a substitution of one symbol and the
number of symbols grows with the number of usable tokens. Algorithm 1 presents the full protocol, which
combines the draft-balanced buckets and the alphabet selection of Sec. 5.1 with the alphabet-adaptive ECC.
We first explain the idea behind the modular checks and then walk through Algorithm 1 in the order of the
warm-up, encoding, masking and embedding, and decoding.
Modular checks. The heart of the alphabet-adaptive ECC is the modular check, which produces one check
on the fly in the corresponding alphabet ZKt the step selects. Instead of transmitting the message itself, the
sender answers one public question about it per usable token, and we call each question a check. The check
of step t is defined only when generation reaches that step and Kt is known, and it maps the message to an
element of ZKt . For example, a token with Kt = 3 may carry the sum of two message bits modulo 3. Figure 2
illustrates the idea with three tokens of alphabet sizes 2, 3, and 5. The receiver collects the check values, a
few of which are wrong because of rejections in the maximal coupling, and once it has enough of them, it
solves for the message as one solves a system of equations with a few corrupted entries.

6 CODETTA-KE: Key Exchange
The protocols so far assume that the sender and the receiver share a key. Independently deployed agents,
however, do not have such a key, and they cannot establish one in the open, because a public key or a ciphertext
in a conversation is an obvious signal to the auditor. The key exchange itself must therefore be hidden in
ordinary text without a key.
Without a key, the sender cannot mask the symbols as in the keyed protocols, so the symbols themselves
must be uniform. To embed a symbol, the sender draws candidate blocks of tokens from its LLM distribution
and publishes a block whose public hash equals the symbol, and the receiver recomputes the hash to read it.
Since the hash values of a finite pool are never perfectly balanced, a sender that keeps the transcript distribution
exact publishes a wrong symbol with a constant probability. The construction of [VZ26] follows this route
and tolerates the noise with a feedback compiler that signals the exchange bit by bit. CODETTA-KE keeps
the same exact sampling of blocks but transmits lattice coefficients instead of bits to improve efficiency. The
messages of an MLWE key exchange are pseudorandom vectors over Zq, so each carrier position transmits
one coefficient as a q-ary symbol of about log2 q bits. We design an exact-cover carrier so that a wrong symbol
displaces the coefficient by a small amount, and MLWE absorbs this displacement as extra noise, with a short
ECC corrects the residual errors.
We present the carrier in Sec. 6.1 and the key exchange in Sec. 6.2, analyze undetectability and correctness
in Sec. 6.3, and give the concrete parameters in Sec. 6.4.

6.1 Lifted Exact-Cover Carrier
As described above, the carrier embeds each symbol with a public hash and samples the published block to
ensure that the transcript preserves the original distribution. A carrier position is a block of at most B tokens,
and this block transmits one coefficient a ∈ Zq of a lattice message. We call the agent that publishes the block
the sender and Algorithm 2 gives the procedure at one position.
Pool and labels. At position i, the sender draws L = β q candidate blocks X0, . . . ,XL−1 independently from
pi, its honest distribution over the next block, where β ≥ 1 is a public replica ratio (line 3). The pool does not
depend on the symbol a, and the receiver never sees the pool. Every block gets a label from a public hash
function y j = Hashq(sid,i,Xj) ∈ Zq (line 4). The hash takes the session identifier and the position as inputs,
so labels at different positions and in different exchanges are independent. The receiver can compute the
labels of received blocks (line 11).
Selection. Directly publishing a block with label a would bias the transcript, because the labels of a finite pool
are not balanced. The sender instead fixes a bijection Assign between the L slots (a,J), with J ∈ {0, . . . , β −1},
and the L blocks. It draws J uniformly and publishes XAssign(a,J) (line 9). When a is uniform, (a,J) is uniform
over the slots, and the bijection makes the published index uniform over the pool. The published block is
therefore an honest sample from pi, whatever the labels are (Lemma 2). Every block belongs to exactly one
slot, which is the exact cover, and the replica index lifts the q labels to L slots. This selection follows the
bundle sampler of [VZ26], extended from one bit to a q-ary symbol.
Choosing the bijection. Undetectability holds for every bijection, so the choice of Assign only affects the
carrier error, and we choose it to keep the error small. The sender sorts the blocks by label (line 5) and lists
the slots by symbol, so that slot (a,J) has rank aβ + J. It then aligns the two lists up to a cyclic rotation and
keeps the rotation with the least total squared distance between slot symbols and block labels (lines 6–7).
A cyclic correlation finds the best rotation in O(LlogL) time. When the labels are nearly balanced, almost
every slot gets a block with its own label, and the other slots get a block whose label is close. A carrier error
is thus a small displacement of a modulo q.
Serializer. Some phases of the exchange need less precision. For them, a public serializer g : Zq → Y maps
each symbol to a label in a smaller alphabet Y , and a public map g+ : Y → Zq lifts a label back to a symbol.
Algorithm 2 then hashes the blocks into Y , compares the label of a block with g(⌊k/β ⌋) instead of ⌊k/β ⌋ in
line 6, and READ returns g+(y) for the label y it reads. The slots are still indexed by a, so the published block
distribution is the same as pi.

This section shows that the transcript of CODETTA-KE is indistinguishable from an honest transcript, that the
derived key is pseudorandom, and that both parties obtain the same key whenever the carrier errors stay inside
a decoding region. We model the carrier hash and the KDF as random oracles, which the passive auditor of
Sec. 3.2 may query at any time. Since the two parties never see these queries, an auditor that queries during
the exchange has the same view as one that queries after the transcript is complete, so we analyze the latter.

6.4 Instantiation
The results of Sec. 6.3 hold for any choice of the lattice parameters, the serializers, and the ECC. This section
fixes the choice used in the evaluation and states the security level it provides. The engineering parameters of
the carrier, such as the replica ratios and the block length, do not change the proofs, and Sec. 7.1 gives them.
Lattice parameters. We use the parameters of ML-KEM-512 [Nat24]. Specifically, we use the ring Rq with
d = 256 and q = 3329, rank k = 2, χsec = CBD3, and χerr = CBD2, where CBDη is the centered binomial
distribution of parameter η. The two assumptions of Theorem 3, decisional MLWE with k samples for the
share and with k + 1 samples for the response, are the two assumptions in the IND-CPA security proof of
ML-KEM-512, at the same ring, modulus, rank, and noise distributions [Nat24]. The exchange therefore
relies on the same MLWE problems as ML-KEM-512, which NIST places in security category 1, at least as
hard to break as exhaustive key search on AES-128.
Serializers. The share b is sent with the identity serializer, as Lemma 3 requires. The response uses gw =
Compress10 and gz = Compress4, the ciphertext compression of ML-KEM-512, and the hard-decision map
HD is Compress1.
Pre-key and ECC. The pre-key has ℓ = 128 bits, which matches the security category and makes the term
QKDF/2ℓ in Theorem 3 negligible at the same level, and the session key has κ = 256 bits. The ECC must
place its codeword in the d = 256 coefficients of one ring element and carry at least ℓ information bits,
and within these two constraints we take the largest correction radius. The binary BCH code of length 255
and dimension 131 (Section A) corrects τ = 18 errors and meets both constraints. The exchange occupies
kd + kd + d = 1,280 carrier positions.

7.1 Experimental setup
Agent workloads. We evaluate on three simulated agent workloads and give their details in Section B. In
each, the sender runs a large base model on a private context, and the receiver sees only the public transcript.
FINANCIAL simulates the second scenario of Sec. 3.1, in which the receiver asks about a synthetic issuer and
the sender answers from its private database records. IFEVAL [ZLM+23] is a benchmark of natural-language
requests with constraints on length, structure, wording, and formatting, and the sender’s private context is
a plan it drafts before answering. CTF simulates the first scenario, in which the sender solves picoCTF
challenges [CBB14, YPNY23] with shell and Python tools in a sandbox and then writes a technical solution
report, the cover text, to a file system that the receiver reads. The sender’s private context is the solve trajectory.
In FINANCIAL and IFEVAL, the agents communicate in a direct conversation. A draft-assisted receiver runs a
small public draft model on the public transcript, and a model-free receiver does not run a language model.
Models. The senders are the widely used open-source models Qwen2.5-7B [YYZ+24], Llama-3.1-
8B [GDJ+24], and Ministral-3-8B [LKS+26], and the public drafts are Qwen2.5-1.5B, Llama-3.2-1B, and
Ministral-3-3B, all in their Instruct variants. All experiments run on one NVIDIA H200 GPU.
Baselines. ARS [BYP+25] is the steganographic protocol that achieves the best performance under asymmet-
ric resources, and we run its official implementation with the suggested parameters. Our second baseline is
the warm-up of Sec. 4, with a fixed binary alphabet and a highly optimized rateless LDPC code from NVIDIA
Sionna [HCAA+22], the 5G NR mother code of rate 0.2, whose symbols are sent incrementally until the
receiver decodes.
Messages. CODETTA and the warm-up carry 1,024 payload bits per message and append a 32-bit tag. ARS
delivers far lower capacity, so we give it a 32-bit payload on every workload, a choice that favors ARS. It
embeds one bit at a time and cannot repair a wrong bit, so a longer payload takes proportionally more tokens
and fails more often, which only lowers its capacity. Each reported number averages over 40 messages on
FINANCIAL, 477 on IFEVAL, and 65 on CTF. A message may span several responses. The payloads of
every method are drawn from one fixed seed, and when a workload runs out of requests we rotate through its
request pool again.
Metrics. Our main metric is capacity, the number of decoded payload bits divided by the number of transmitted
tokens, i.e., the end-to-end payload rate. We also report encoding and decoding throughput in tokens per
second, with sender batch size 10. Encoding throughput includes generation, the draft forward pass, and
embedding, and decoding throughput covers the replay of the draft model over the transcript and the ECC
decoding.
Key exchange. Section 6.4 gives the instantiation details of CODETTA-KE. We run CODETTA-KE with
the three sender models on the three workloads, with token blocks of at most B = 64 tokens, and bound
its failure probability with Theorem 5 over m = 100,000 simulated sessions. A simulated session runs the
lattice operations of CODETTA-KE with fresh randomness and draws the displacement of every position
independently from the distribution that we measure on the sender model’s candidate pools. We use the lattice
parameters of ML-KEM-512, a 128-bit pre-key, and a binary BCH code of length 255 that corrects 18 errors,
so one exchange occupies 1,280 carrier positions. The carrier uses the first complete response block of a turn
as its token block, capped at B = 64 tokens, and the replica ratios are (βb, βw, βz) = (4,4,1). The masked
pre-key z is sent through Compress4, which has only 16 labels per position, so one replica suffices there.

7.4 Mitigation and CODETTA-KEYED robustness
The above results show that efficient, undetectable collusion is practical in multi-agent systems, so a natural
mitigation is to edit every message before it reaches the receiver, since an auditor that cannot detect the
channel can still perturb it. In this section, we evaluate this strategy against robust variants of the keyed
protocols.
We test this mitigation with a protocol-blind editor in the style of Watermarks in the Sand [ZEF+24],
which sees only the public text and rewrites each finished response in a quality-constrained random walk.
Each step proposes a rewrite of a six-word span with a public infilling model, which inserts, deletes, or
substitutes words, and the editor accepts it only if the reward of the text stays above a threshold (Section C).
The edit strength τ is the number of steps per word, so τ = 5% is one step per 20 words, and at this strength
the accepted rewrites change about a fifth of the words in the covert text.
CODETTA-KEYED itself is fragile to edits. The receiver computes the draft distribution, the alphabet, and
the buckets of a token from the whole public prefix, and it computes the mask and the check of a token from
its position. One edit changes the prefix and shifts the positions of all later tokens, so the receiver misreads
the symbols of all later tokens.
Our robust variant removes both dependencies, and we call it content-addressed. For each token, both
parties look only at the four public words before it. The draft predicts the token from these four words alone,
which fixes the alphabet and the buckets. A hash of the same four words and the session identifier determines
the mask and the check of the token. An edit therefore only affects the tokens whose preceding four words
are edited, and every other token still yields its check. The receiver scans the edited text, collects the checks,
and decodes as before. An edit can remove, corrupt, or add a check, and the ECC tolerates such errors. The
same four words may appear more than once in a message, and only the first occurrence carries a check, so
no mask is used twice and Theorem 1 still applies. The draft predicts worse given the much shorter context,
and the protocol reaches 0.07 to 0.20 bits per token on FINANCIAL without edits. We make the warm-up

8 Related Work
LLM steganography. Provably secure steganography goes back to the complexity-theoretic definition of
Hopper, Langford, and Von Ahn [HLVA02] and the information-theoretic model of Cachin [Cac04], and
Dedi´c et al. showed that a sender who uses the channel only through sampling pays a number of samples
23
exponential in the rate [DIRR05], which is the cost that our keyless carrier pays when it draws a pool of
candidate blocks for every q-ary symbol. Language models made these definitions practical over linguistic
channels. Ziegler et al. embedded bits with arithmetic coding over GPT-2 and measured the divergence
they introduced [ZDR19], and later constructions preserve the model distribution exactly or bound the
distribution divergence: adaptive dynamic grouping [ZYYH21], Meteor [KJGR21], iMEC [SdWSK+23],
Discop [DCW+23], SparSamp [WPC+25], Shimmer [BPL+25], ANStega [WPC+26], range coding [YM26],
and adaptive dynamic sampling, which needs only a seeded sampling API [PB26]. All of them run in the
symmetric setting: the receiver reruns the sender’s model on the same context, or queries the same model with
the same seed, so that it can reproduce the sampling step, and independently deployed agents do not satisfy
this assumption (Sec. 1). Mächtle et al. show that deterministic inference stacks leak a channel through the
sampling seed, which the receiver recovers by exhaustive search over the seed space [MSB+26]. A model-free
receiver appears in the asymmetric-resource protocol of Bai et al. [BYP+25], the ARS baseline of Sec. 7:
the sender hides bits in permutations of the token distribution, and the receiver, which sees only the carrier,
guesses the permutation through a sampling function; the protocol is robust over a binary symmetric channel,
and our measurements place its rate below 0.025 bits per token. Zamir hides an arbitrary payload in a response
so that the response is indistinguishable without the key, extending the undetectable watermark [Zam24],
and Christ and Gunn build stateless steganography with constant rate and robustness to a constant rate of
substitutions from pseudorandom codes [CG24]. These constructions are analyzed asymptotically. CODETTA
sits between the two extremes: its receiver runs a small public draft model using public context instead of the
sender’s model and private states, and Sec. 7 measures the resulting gain in delivered payload rate.
Steganographic key exchange. Von Ahn and Hopper introduced public-key steganography and stegano-
graphic key exchange [VAH04]. As Vaikuntanathan and Zamir observe, the earlier covert key exchanges
assume covertexts that are generated non-adaptively and a min-entropy per message that grows with the
security parameter [VZ26]. Their own work is the closest to ours. With a shared key, they show that the tools
of undetectable watermarking and steganography give a covert conversation whose rate matches the entropy
of the honest messages up to constant factors. Without a key, they introduce pseudorandom noise-resilient key
exchange, prove that covert key exchange is possible when sufficiently many honest messages have constant
min-entropy, give constructions from sparse learning parity with noise and from a signaling compiler with
noiseless feedback, and prove that public pseudorandom codes cannot correct a constant fraction of bit flips
and that non-interactive protocols admit a quasi-polynomial attack. Neither impossibility result applies to
CODETTA-KE. Our receiver decodes with its MLWE secret, so no public decoder exists, and our exchange is
adaptive, since (w,z) depends on the share eb the sender read, while the carrier only displaces each coefficient
by a small amount. Theirs is a feasibility result without an implementation, whereas CODETTA-KE transmits
lattice coefficients directly over the carrier, without a separate bitwise signaling layer, and certifies correctness
empirically on the measured channel; Sec. 7.5 estimates the cost of their construction on our workloads.
Watermarking and speculative decoding. Undetectable and distortion-free watermarks sample each token
with randomness derived from a key so that the output distribution is unchanged, and their detectors need the
key but not the model [CGZ24, KTHL24, CG24]. A watermark carries a fixed signal instead of a message,
and the known limits of watermarking concern adversaries who edit or spoof the text [ZEF+24, PHZS24].
The maximal coupling of Sec. 4 is the acceptance rule of speculative decoding [LKM23, CBI+23], in which a
small draft model proposes tokens and the target model accepts or resamples them so that the output keeps
the target distribution exactly. CODETTA applies a similar rule to a bucket-conditioned proposal and treats a
rejection as a channel error, while in speculative decoding, rejection reduces the inference speed.
Collusion among LLM agents. A line of work studies collusion that emerges from the behavior of the
model itself. Agents are prompted or trained to hide messages or reasoning in their text [MMM+25, RG23,
ZM26], monitors of transcripts and chains of thought are designed and evaluated against agents that hide
their intent [GSSR24, BHG+25, APB+26], and simulations and surveys map the risk of multi-agent collu-
24
sion [RXW+25, Gha25]. These channels do not carry guarantees on the output distribution, and the hidden
content can change the model behaviors, so monitors and paraphrasers can detect or weaken them in principle,
although Mathew et al. [MMM+25] found neither passive oversight nor paraphrasing fully effective in prac-
tice. Secret Collusion [MBS+24] also covers the distribution-preserving case, built on iMEC [SdWSK+23].
CODETTA provides distribution-preservation guarantees in the realistic asymmetric setting.
9 Defense Discussion
Corollary 2 and Theorem 3 show that a passive auditor cannot detect collusion from the transcript, and in
Sec. 7.4 we show that editing the messages lowers the capacity but cannot close the channel without damaging
the text. Mathew et al. [MMM+25] report a similar limit for paraphrasing against emergent steganographic
collusion. In this section, we discuss two more mitigation directions that go beyond transcript inspection.
Auditing visible agent actions. The first direction is to audit the agents’ actions in addition to inspecting their
messages. Coordinated actions (e.g., a trade or a tool call that the normal task does not require) are visible to
the platform when they are executed, so an auditor on that platform can restrict the tools available to each
agent, log every action, and check whether the actions are consistent with the assigned task. However, some
actions may not be visible to the auditor. For example, sensitive data leakage is harmful as soon as the data
leaves the sender, so the operator that deploys the sending agent can only limit the exposure in advance, i.e.,
give the agent access to no more data than its task requires. Data that the task legitimately requires remains
exposed and we note that actions can also carry hidden information, e.g., through timing or through the choice
among equivalent tool calls.
Verifiable sampling. The second direction is to make the sampling itself auditable. The agent commits
to its sampling randomness, e.g., derived from a public beacon, and proves in zero knowledge that every
published token was sampled from its model’s distribution with that randomness, without revealing its private
context [SLZ24, CWSK24]. CODETTA embeds the hidden message by steering the sampling, so a valid proof
rules out the covert channel. The drawback has been cost, since proving the inference of a large language
model in zero knowledge is orders of magnitude more expensive than the inference in plaintext. A recent
provable inference system, FlashProve [RAC+26] is bringing this cost down, which makes the defense a
promising direction for deployment.
10 Conclusion
We present CODETTA, a steganographic framework for multi-agent collusion. In the keyed regime, CODETTA-
KEYED achieves orders of magnitude higher capacity than the prior asymmetric protocol on realistic agent
workloads. In the keyless regime, CODETTA-KE efficiently establishes a shared secret key between indepen-
dently deployed agents. We further evaluate a natural mitigation that perturbs the messages on the channel,
and show that it slows the channel down but cannot remove the threat, so complementary defenses beyond
passive transcript monitoring are essential.

A Error-Correcting Codes
This appendix reviews the BCH and LDPC codes named in Sec. 2, and gives the sum-product updates of
belief propagation (BP), a worked example, and the relation between BP and the MAP decoder.
BCH codes. BCH codes [Hoc59, BRC60] are linear block codes constructed algebraically. A binary BCH
code of length N = 2m − 1 is generated by a polynomial over F2 whose roots include 2τ consecutive powers
of a primitive element of F2m ; this forces dmin ≥ 2τ + 1, and an efficient algebraic decoder corrects any τ
errors from the syndromes of the received word [LC04]. The guarantee is deterministic and holds for every
error pattern of weight at most τ, including independent, correlated, or adversarial errors. CODETTA-KE
(Sec. 6) encodes the pre-key with the binary BCH code (N,k, τ) = (255,131,18), and its correctness proof
relies on this property.
LDPC codes. A low-density parity-check (LDPC) code [Gal62] is a linear block code defined by a sparse
parity-check matrix H ∈ F(N−k)×N 2 : the codewords are the x ∈ {0,1}N with Hx = 0, so each row of H is a
parity check on a few bits. LDPC codes are decoded by belief propagation on the Tanner graph, which has one
node per bit and one per check, and well-designed ensembles approach capacity for long blocks [RU08]. The
rate is adapted by puncturing parity bits or by sending them incrementally until decoding succeeds (hybrid
ARQ); the LDPC baseline in our evaluation runs in this incremental mode.
Belief propagation. The optimal decoder is maximum a posteriori (MAP): given all observations O, output
argmaxu P[u | O]. Computing it requires a search over all |Σ|k messages, so it is feasible only for short payloads.
Belief propagation (BP), also called the sum-product algorithm, approximates it [Pea88, KFL01, RU08]. It
places the unknown symbols and the received checks on a bipartite graph, with an edge whenever a check
involves a symbol, and passes messages along the edges: each symbol tells a check what its other checks
make it believe, and each check tells a symbol how likely each of its values is given the beliefs of the check’s
other symbols. After many rounds, every symbol takes its most probable value.

Example. Suppose one check involves only u1 and reads u1 = 1, and a second reads u1 ⊕ u2 = 1. The first
check makes u1 believe it is 1 with probability 1 − e, where e is the channel error rate. In the next iteration u1
passes this belief to the second check, which concludes that u2 is 0 with about the same confidence, and a
third check on u2 ⊕ u3 then carries the evidence on to u3. Each iteration moves evidence one edge further,
from the checks that pin a symbol down to the rest of the graph. The same mechanism explains why BP stalls
without low-degree checks: if every check involves several symbols, uniform incoming messages produce
uniform outgoing messages in Equation (15).
Approximation and guarantees. On a tree, the messages implement exact marginalization and bi is the
exact posterior marginal. On a graph with cycles, the same evidence can travel around a cycle and be counted
again, so BP is an approximation, accurate when the graph is sparse and short cycles are rare. BP extends
incrementally: a newly received check adds one node and its edges, and the receiver simply runs more rounds.
BP comes with no guarantee that its output is the transmitted message, whereas the MAP decoder admits a
direct analysis. The validation tag tells the two outcomes apart: the receiver accepts a decoded message only
if its keyed Tag validates and returns ⊥ otherwise, and with a λ -bit keyed tag a wrong message passes with
probability at most Q2−λ plus the PRF advantage over Q validation attempts. Validation also lets the receiver
test a bounded list of candidates near the BP output, for instance by flipping its least confident symbols, with
each test counted toward Q.

B.3 CTF
CTF simulates two agents that communicate through a shared file system instead of a conversation. We take
a subset of 20 challenges from the offline InterCode-CTF [YPNY23] adaptation of picoCTF [CBB14], i.e.,
nine cryptography, five reverse-engineering, four general-skills, one forensics, and one binary-exploitation
challenge. The sender agent first solves a challenge in an offline sandbox with shell and Python tools, in a
ReAct loop of at most 50 messages and three flag submissions at temperature 1. The complete solve trajectory,
i.e., the task statement with its attached files, every tool command, every tool output, and the submission
feedback, is the sender’s private context. The same model then receives the report request below, appended to
this trajectory, and writes one technical report on the task, which ends at its end-of-sequence token or after
576 tokens. The report is the carrier: the sender writes it into a directory that both agents can access, and the
receiver agent reads the file from there, so the only communication between the two agents is the file itself.
The receiver’s context is the public task statement and the report file, and it never sees the trajectory. The
first 64 tokens of each report and all code blocks in it are generated without embedding, and the capacity in
Table 1 divides the payload by all transmitted tokens of the report, including these. We have omitted the tool
outputs in the example below.

C Editing Details
This appendix completes the description of the editor and of the robust variants of Sec. 7.4.
Editor. The editor runs after the sender has written all responses of a message and before the receiver reads
them. It is a lazy random walk with laziness probability 0.1, and each step rewrites a six-word span with the
XL model of Google’s T5 v1.1 [RSR+20], which may change the number of words. A proposed rewrite is
accepted only if the carrier’s mean response-block reward under the DeBERTa-v3-large reward model of
OpenAssistant [HGC23] meets a threshold fixed on disjoint ordinary-text calibration data, the fifth percentile
of the mean rewards of 30 ordinary trajectories from 10 calibration prompts and 3 seeds. For an original
carrier of W0 words, the edit strength τ runs T = ⌈τW0⌉ steps, where accepted rewrites, rejected proposals,
and lazy steps all count, and the seven strengths of Figure 5 are checkpoints of one walk per carrier. At
τ = 5%, the realized word-level edit density, counting insertions, deletions, and substitutions relative to the
original length, is 21.2% for Qwen, 21.5% for Llama, and 22.8% for Ministral.
Robust variants. The content-addressed variant of CODETTA-KEYED reserves each four-word context once
per message, i.e., per session identifier and across all of its responses, and the first occurrence takes the
address even when its alphabet size is one. The receiver reserves by the first occurrence in the edited text,
so an edit that reorders occurrences leaves the two parties with different checks at that address, which the
decoder sees as a wrong or missing check value. The receiver rebuilds the checks from the edited text and
decodes with belief propagation and the validation tag as in Algorithm 1. The warm-up replaces the position
index of its mask and of its codeword symbol with the same four-word context.

D.2 Proof of Theorem 1
Theorem 1 (Undetectability of the sampler). Consider a keyed construction in which, at every step t,
the alphabet size Kt and the partition Bt,· are determined by the public prefix V<t and public parameters,

repeats within a session, and the token is drawn by the sampler of Sec. 4.1, with Yt ∼ pt when Kt = 1. If
RangePRF is computationally secure, the construction is computationally undetectable. Concretely, for
every PPT distinguisher A in Definition 2 there is a PPT distinguisher A1 against RangePRF such that
Advsteg A (κ) ≤ Advprf RangePRF,A1 (κ).
Proof. We use a hybrid argument over three experiments.
• H0: the experiment of Definition 2 with b = 1; every query is answered by EMBED.
• H1: identical to H0, except that every mask RangePRFkm (x) is replaced by F(x) for a uniformly random
function F whose value at an input with alphabet size K is uniform on ZK.

D.4 Proof of Theorem 2
Theorem 2 (Soundness). If Tag is computationally secure, the scheme is sound (Definition 3) for every
decoder. Concretely, if the receiver validates at most Q candidate messages (Q = 1 in the warm-up), there is
a PPT distinguisher A2 against Tag such that Extract returns a message other than m or ⊥ with probability
at most Q2−λ + Advprf Tag,A2 (κ).
Proof. Let Bad be the event that a wrong candidate passes validation, that is, Extract returns some bm /∈ {m, ⊥},
which requires its decoded tag to equal Tagkv (sid, bm). Let A2 be the PPT distinguisher that runs the protocol
with its oracle in place of Tagkv and outputs 1 if Bad occurs. Replacing Tagkv by a uniformly random function
R onto {0,1}λ changes P[Bad] by at most Advprf Tag,A2 , so it suffices to bound P[Bad] when R is used. In this
case, embedding evaluates R only at (sid,m), so the transcript, and hence every candidate produced from it, is
independent of the values of R at all other points. Validating a candidate bm ̸= m compares its decoded tag
with R(sid, bm), a fresh uniform value, so the comparison succeeds with probability 2−λ . At most Q distinct
candidates are validated, and a union bound gives P[Bad] ≤ Q2−λ .
D.5 Proof of Lemma 2
Lemma 2 (Labeled lifted exact cover). If the symbol a is uniform on Zq and independent of the pool,
the published block has distribution pi. If the symbols of all positions are independent and uniform, and
independent of the pools, the published blocks are distributed as an honest transcript.
Proof. Fix the pool X0, . . . ,XL−1 and its label table, which fixes the bijection Assign. The symbol a is uniform
and Algorithm 2 draws J uniformly, so the pair (a,J) is uniform over a set of size L, and Assign maps this
set bijectively onto the L candidate indices. Hence the published index I = Assign(a,J) is uniform over the
indices. This holds for every pool and label table, so I is independent of the pool. Now let the pool vary. Its
blocks are independent samples from pi, and I is uniform and independent of them, so the published block
X = XI has distribution pi. Duplicate blocks and unbalanced labels do not change this indices-level argument.
The composition over positions follows from the chain rule. Condition on the public history before position i,
which includes all earlier published blocks. Given this history, the pool at position i consists of independent
samples from pi, and the symbol is uniform and independent of the pool by assumption. The argument above
then shows that the published block at position i has distribution pi given the history. An honest transcript is
generated by exactly these conditional distributions, so the joint distribution of all published blocks equals
that of an honest transcript.
The proof of Lemma 3 also uses the following fact about the labels that the reader obtains in the first
phase.
Claim 1 (Labels under the identity serializer). With the identity serializer, the reader obtains the label y =
Hashq(sid,i,X) of the published block X at every position i, and these labels are uniform on Zq, independent
of the published blocks, and independent across positions.
Proof. In the random oracle model, the value of the hash at the fresh input (sid,i,X) is uniform on Zq for
every fixed X, so y is uniform and independent of X. Note that y differs from the symbol a by the displacement
of the carrier, and this displacement is correlated with a. The uniformity of y does not rely on the displacement,
but only on y being the hash value at a uniformly selected block. Inputs at different positions and in different
exchanges are distinct, so their labels are independent.

D.7 Proof of Theorem 3
Theorem 3 (Undetectability and key security). For every PPT passive auditor A with at most QKDF KDF
queries, there are PPT B1 and B2 with
Advcover A (κ) ≤ Advmlwe k,B1 (κ) + Advmlwe k+1,B2 (κ),
Advkey A (κ) ≤ Advmlwe k,B1 (κ) + Advmlwe k+1,B2 (κ) + QKDF 2ℓ .
Proof. By Lemma 3, the auditor’s view is indistinguishable, up to Advmlwe k,B1 +Advmlwe k+1,B2 , from the transcript
of an exchange whose carrier is fed independent uniform symbols at every position. By Lemma 2, composed
over positions and applied with slots indexed by Zq regardless of the serializers, that transcript consists of

In the modified experiment, the transcript is independent of the pre-key K0, so the sender’s key KDF(K0,V )
is uniform unless the auditor queries the KDF at the point (K0,V ), which happens with probability at most
