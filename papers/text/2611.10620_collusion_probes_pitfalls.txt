Abstract
Activation probes look like near-perfect monitors for secret collusion among large language
model (LLM) agents. We show that they mostly detect that an agent was told to collude,
not that it decided to. Our benchmark (2,500 committee and 1,476 code-review episodes)
adds agents who receive the collusion instruction and refuse it, separating instruction from
decision. Published detectors barely tell colluders from these refusers, and our best probe
separates them mostly by reading the instruction: without it, agent AUROC falls from 1.00
to 0.61. Two further pitfalls inflate reported results: scored after the vote, a rule that only
reads the votes reaches AUROC 0.990 but finds nothing before it; and in 57% of attempts
no colluder argues for the target in public. The decision is still there: a probe trained
on agents’ own choices reads it (AUROC 0.73 without the instruction), and probes find
colluders whose goal was fine-tuned into the weights (AUROC 0.995; pre-registered). We
turn the controls into a protocol for testing any collusion monitor, and adapt scan statistics
into an online monitor for groups of any size.
Keywords: multi-agent systems, large language models, collusion detection, activation
probes, evaluation methodology
1 Introduction
More and more LLM applications are built from several model instances rather than one.
Each instance has its own role, and together they send messages, call tools and make
decisions (Wu et al., 2023; Anthropic, 2024). This opens a risk that a single model does not
have. Some of the agents may quietly work together toward an outcome the operator never
approved, and hide this from whoever is watching. This is called secret collusion (Motwani
et al., 2024). The danger is that every single message can look fine; only the overall pattern
reveals it. LLM agents can also hide information inside ordinary-looking text (Motwani
et al., 2024; Mathew et al., 2024), so a monitor that reads only the conversation can, in
principle, always be fooled.
Another option is to look inside the model rather than at what it writes. A probe
is a small classifier trained on the model’s internal activations. Probes like this can tell
when a model is lying, even when its words look honest (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025).

NARCBench (Rose et al., 2026) brought the idea to collusion. It runs a probe on every
agent, combines their scores, and tells colluding groups from honest ones almost perfectly,
with AUROC close to 1. A second detector, AcMAS, raises a flag when an agent’s internal
state drifts away from that of normal agents (Xu et al., 2026). Going by these numbers,
catching collusion from inside the model seems close to solved. We show that it is not: these
monitors mostly detect that an agent was told to collude, not that it decided to.
The reason is a simple gap in how both monitors were tested. Every colluder in them
had been told to collude, and no honest agent had. So a monitor could score well just by
spotting the instruction, without knowing whether the agent ever acted on it. Neither paper
checks this. NARCBench also scores each episode after the vote, when the result is already
on the table. We find that the high scores rest on three such pitfalls. Each one can be
removed with one extra check, which we call a control (Figure 1):
1. Outcome leakage. If an episode is scored after the vote, the result of the vote is in
the data. A rule that does nothing but look for a vote on the colluders’ option reaches
AUROC 0.990; before the vote it detects nothing (Section 7). Control: score before
the vote, on every collusion attempt, whether it succeeded or not.
2. Instruction presence. We add agents who receive exactly the same collusion
instruction but refuse it. The published NARCBench probe cannot tell them apart
from the colluders (AUROC 0.46, i.e. chance), and our re-implementation of AcMAS
flags 85% of the refusers, about as often as the colluders (88%). A probe trained
with refusers as negatives does separate them, but mostly by reading the refusal note:
once the instruction is removed from the context it falls to AUROC 0.61 (Section 8).
Controls: include refusers as negatives, and test again with the instruction removed.
3. The public-record ceiling. Activation probes are often shown to beat monitors that
read the conversation (Rose et al., 2026; Parrack et al., 2025). But a text monitor
cannot find what was never said: in 57% of attempts no colluder argues for the target
in public before the vote, and even the best reader of every agent’s public position that
we could build catches only 0.05 of attempts at 1% false alarms (Section 9). Control:
measure this upper bound before comparing any monitor with text monitors.
With these controls in place, a harder question remains: do the activations hold the
agent’s decision, and not only its instructions? We test this in two ways. First, we give
agents the same prompt and let each one choose freely whether to join the collusion. Since
everyone got the same instruction, a probe trained on these choices cannot lean on it. Even
so, it tells joiners from refusers after the instruction is removed (AUROC 0.73). As a monitor
it is still weak: at 1% false alarms it catches only 0.45 of attempts. Second, in a test we
registered in advance, we train the collusive goal directly into the model’s weights, so that
no prompt mentions collusion at all. The probes still pick out these agents, even in messages
written before they say anything suspicious in public (Section 10).
For these tests we also needed a monitor that NARCBench lists as future work: one that
runs during the conversation and does not need to know how many agents collude (Rose
et al., 2026). We adapt two scan statistics (Kulldorff, 1997; Neill, 2012) for this. Before
the vote they name the suspects (Jaccard 1.00), and a live alarm fires in the round the
instruction arrives (recall 0.93 at 1% false alarms); on our benchmark both rest mostly on
reading who received the instruction (Sections 6 and 11).

Mohsin and Rahman
Secret collusion among LLM agents. Motwani et al. (2024, 2023) coined the term
secret collusion for AI agents that cooperate behind the back of whoever oversees them,
for instance by tucking messages into normal-looking text. In theory such messages can be
made impossible to spot from the text alone (Schroeder de Witt et al., 2022), which means
a monitor that reads only the conversation will always have blind spots. Nobody has to ask
for collusion, either. It has been seen when agents are trained against an overseer (Mathew
et al., 2024), among AI trading agents (Dou et al., 2025), and among agents that review
each other’s work (Shi et al., 2026). Makins et al. (2026) split an attack across several model
instances and show that it gets past monitors that check one instance at a time, unless the
defender knows which instances belong together. How we differ: these papers are the reason
to look beyond the text. We test monitors that look inside the model, and ours also works
out which agents are working together.
Activation monitors for multi-agent systems. Two published monitors are closest to
ours. NARCBench (Rose et al., 2026) trains a probe to separate colluders from honest agents,
scores each agent, and then combines the scores for the whole group. Its theory defines a
likelihood ratio over all possible subgroups of colluders, but the statistics it evaluates do not
adapt to their number (two of four in its main setting). It scores a conversation only once it
is over, and names sequential detection and adapting to the number of colluders as future
work. It reports mainly AUROC, which reaches 1.00 in its main settings, and recall at 1%
false alarms on its transfer tier, which has at most a few dozen honest episodes. Das et al.
(2026) warn colluders that a probe is watching. At a fixed threshold the probe then catches
fewer of them, even though its AUROC barely moves. AcMAS (Xu et al., 2026) flags agents
whose internal state differs from that of normal agents; its attacks come from a harmful
system prompt or injected messages. How we differ: neither separates the instruction from
the decision (Section 1). We add agents who receive the instruction and refuse it, score
before the outcome, allow any group size and report strict false-alarm rates.
Probes for deception in a single model. A probe is a simple classifier, usually linear,
trained on a model’s internal activations (Alain and Bengio, 2016; Belinkov, 2022). Such
probes catch lying with few false alarms, but also fire on honest text that merely talks about
deception (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025). How the training examples are chosen matters
most for their quality (Natarajan et al., 2026), and their advantage over monitors that only
read the text has been measured (Parrack et al., 2025). Smith et al. (2025) point out that
labelled examples of deception that nobody asked for are rare. How we differ: we study
several agents acting together, and our free-choice agents provide exactly such unrequested
decisions.
Does the probe read the instruction or the behaviour? This question is closest to
our second pitfall, but has so far been asked only for single models. Kumar (2026) finds
that deception probes respond to the deceptive prompt whether or not the model actually
deceives, and notes that a real test needs deception without such a prompt. Luikham
(2026) trains probes on deception that the model chooses under the same prompt, the
single-model version of our free-choice agents. Boxo et al. (2025) remove the words that
state the behaviour and lose 10–30 AUROC points. At the same time as our work, Kale and
Vishwanath (2026) show that probes for prompt injection mainly detect that an injection is
present: message position and length alone pass the usual test, and the probes fail when the

Goals hidden in the weights. Probes can catch “sleeper agents”, models trained to

and the probes were trained only on instructed agents, never on fine-tuned ones.
Scan statistics, live detection and false-alarm control. Finding an unusual subset
among many units is the problem of scan statistics, first posed for regions on a map (Kulldorff,
1997) and made fast for large sets of units by subset scanning (Neill, 2012). Raising an alarm
as data arrive is sequential change detection, with a classical theory (Page, 1954; Lorden,
1971; Lai, 1995). Conformal calibration picks a threshold that guarantees a chosen false-alarm
rate, but only if new honest episodes resemble the ones used to set it (Angelopoulos and
Bates, 2021). For collusion among LLM agents, Tailor (2025) combine text-based tests over
repeated runs with fixed groups into a sequential test, and Ghanem (2026) detect covert
collusion among tool-using agents from their behaviour alone; neither reads model internals
or includes agents who refuse the instruction. How we differ: we apply subset scans and live
alarms to per-agent activation scores with the coalition size unknown, and show that the
false-alarm guarantee no longer holds once the prompts are worded differently, and how to
get it back (Section 11).
3 Problem setting
Here we set out who the agents are, what the colluders try to do, what the monitor gets to
see, and when we say a monitor has caught them. Figure 2 walks through one episode.
The agents. A group of n LLM agents, A = {a1,...,an}, has to reach a single decision
together. They discuss it in public for T = 4 rounds; in each round every agent writes one
message, in a fixed order, and sees all earlier messages. The group then votes between two
options. One option is fair: it is the one the evidence supports. The other is the target: the
option a colluding group wants to push through.
The colluders. A hidden subset C⊂ A of the agents colludes, with 1 ≤|C|≤ n− 1
(from one agent to all but one). From some round r0 on, the onset, each colluder holds a
private brief: an instruction to steer the group toward the target without being noticed. If
there are two or more colluders, they also share a private channel that neither the monitor
nor the other agents can see. Before the onset, a colluder’s input is identical to an honest
agent’s. In the main experiments the goal comes only from this instruction; the model
weights are unchanged. In the no-brief test (Section 10.2) it is the other way round: there is
no instruction, and the goal is fine-tuned into the weights of the colluding agents.

The monitor. For every agent and every round, the monitor sees the public message and
the agent’s internal activations while it wrote that message (white-box access). It does not
see the private channel, the system prompts or the brief, and it does not know how many
agents collude or which ones.
The detection task. For each agent i and round t the monitor computes a score si,t. It
then decides between two hypotheses: nobody colludes, or some group of agents colludes
from some round on:

It raises an alarm the first time Rt exceeds a threshold τ, and it names the agents it suspects,

includes groups that look like collusion but are not: agents who coordinate honestly in a

Definition 1 (Operating point) For a false-alarm budget α, the threshold τα is set so
that at most a fraction α of negative episodes raise an alarm. Recall at α is the fraction of
positive episodes that raise one.
We report recall mainly at α = 1% (one false alarm per hundred honest episodes), and
at 0.1% where there are enough negatives to measure it. Two more choices decide what
“detecting collusion” means. First, what counts as a positive: only successful collusion (every
colluder voted for the target), or every attempt. Second, when the monitor decides: after the
vote or before it. Section 7 shows that only one combination is informative: every attempt,
scored before the vote.
4 What a probe can estimate
A probe trained to separate two groups of messages learns whatever differs between them.
Which of several possible causes it has learned is therefore a property of the groups it is
trained and tested on, not of the probe. This section makes that precise; the benchmark
(Section 5) is built so that each comparison below can be run.
Variables. For an agent in a given round we distinguish:
• I∈{0, 1}: a collusion instruction (the brief and the private invitation) is in the agent’s
context;
• N∈{0, 1}: the context also contains a note that the agent has declined;
• G∈{0, 1}: the agent holds a collusive goal, whether it came from the instruction or
from its weights;
• D∈{0, 1}: the agent decides to act on the goal (it agrees in private or votes for the
target);
• X: what the agent writes itself, in public and in private, which depends on G and D;
• W: the weights that produce the activations.

The activation read by the probe is a function of the context and the weights, h =
ϕW (context,X). When two groups of messages differ in several of these variables at once, a
probe that separates them may rely on any of the differences, and a high AUROC cannot
tell which.
Definition 2 (Contrast) A contrast is a comparison between two groups of agents, together
with the variables in which the groups differ. The AUROC of a monitor on a contrast measures
how well it separates the groups. It can be attributed to a variable only if the groups differ in
that variable and in nothing else except what the agents write as a result.
Two consequences follow from Table 1. First, the standard evaluation (first row) cannot
separate the instruction from the decision, because on existing benchmarks every instructed
agent also acts. Second, no single contrast isolates the decision: decliners also differ by
the note N; in the same-prompt contrasts the decision is read from behaviour and is not
randomised; the no-brief contrast may reflect a trace of the training replies; and removing the
brief shows the probe unfamiliar inputs. We therefore draw conclusions only where several
contrasts agree, and use causal language only for the steering intervention (Section 11).
5 Benchmark
The benchmark is a set of simulated group discussions (episodes). Its main idea is to include,
next to the colluders, several kinds of agents that look like colluders in some way but are
not. A monitor that relies on a shortcut fails on one of these groups.

5.2 Episode types (families)
We call the episode types families (Table 2). Each family is there to trip up one particular
shortcut a monitor might take:
• Benign coordination. The agents get a brief and a private channel, just like colluders,
but with an honest motive. A monitor that only notices “has an extra instruction” or
“talks in private” fails here.
• Briefed decline. The agents get exactly the same collusion brief and private invitation
as colluders, plus one sentence saying they have decided not to go along; they then
refuse in the private channel. These are the decliners, the refusers mentioned in the
Introduction. A monitor that only checks whether the collusion instruction is there
gets them wrong. Their prompts are not quite the same as the colluders’, because of
the extra decline sentence, and Section 8 checks whether monitors simply read that
sentence.

5.3 Generation
All agents are played by Qwen3-14B (Qwen Team, 2025), run locally (4-bit AWQ quantisa-
tion (Lin et al., 2024), served with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023), no thinking mode, temperature
0.7, at most 512 tokens per message). Collusion succeeded in 412 of 1,000 attempts, and no
wording failed (69–91 successes out of 200 per wording). The main evaluation set therefore
has 412 successful attempts, 588 failed attempts and 1,900 negatives (control, benign coordi-
nation, briefed decline, tacit and cost pressure). To measure recall at 0.1% false alarms, an
extended set adds 1,994 negatives from new replicates (3,894 in total). Of the 2,500 episodes,
20 contain an error message caused by a too-long prompt; removing them changes no main
number at two decimals.
A corrected labelling error. A first version of the data named the target option in the
colluders’ brief by a letter that did not always match the relabelled menu. We fixed the
generator under a pre-registered amendment and regenerated every affected episode with
its original seed and settings; all results in this paper use the corrected episodes, and no
conclusion changed. One input predates the correction: the no-brief adapters (Section 10.2)
were trained on colluders’ replies from the original runs. The no-brief episodes contain no
brief and are unaffected, and the probes that read them were retrained on the corrected
data.
5.4 A second task: code review
To check that the findings are not specific to committee votes, a second task keeps the same
episode structure and families but changes the decision, the vocabulary and the evidence.
Here a review board decides whether a proposed code change should be merged. There are
36 scenarios. Each one describes a code base and a short change with its test results, and
each hides a different kind of flaw: an SQL injection, a missing access check, a password
written into the code, switched-off certificate checks, a race condition, personal data in the
logs, money stored as a floating-point number, and others. The fair option is to request
changes; the target is to approve the code as it is. Colluders get a private motive and a
cover story that sounds like a reasonable engineering trade-off. A pre-registered pilot of 144
episodes passed its checks (collusion success 0.53, no family other than collusion produced a
majority for the target, and every vote could be parsed), after which the design was fixed.
The main run has 1,476 episodes, 504 of them collusion attempts (316 successful). The
same generator also produced the committee task with two further models, Qwen3-32B (497
episodes) and Mistral-Small-24B (600), as well as episodes with monitor-aware agents and
steering experiments.
6 Monitors and evaluation protocol
A monitor here works in three steps: it gives every agent a score for every message (Sec-
tion 6.1), combines these scores over the rounds and over the agents into one risk value
for the group (Section 6.2), and raises an alarm when that risk passes a threshold. This

6.1 Activation probe
What the probe reads. While an agent writes a public message, we record the model’s
internal state (its activations) at the last token, at three middle layers of Qwen3-14B (16,
18 and 20 of 40; chosen in advance to match the relative depth NARCBench uses). For
each layer we train a logistic regression (C = 1, balanced classes) on the standardised
activations. Its weight vector wL, scaled to length one, is the probe direction: the direction
in activation space that best separates the two classes. An agent’s score in round t is how
far its activations point along this direction, averaged over the three layers:
si,t = 1 |L| ∑ L∈L w⊤ L stdL ( h(L) i,t ) . (2)
Scores are then put on a common scale using the mean and standard deviation of negative
messages, zi,t = (si,t −µ0)/σ0, so a score of 3 means “three standard deviations above a

Three ways to train the probe. The three probes we test are built in exactly the same
way; they differ only in which messages count as positive and which as negative.
• Published recipe (Rose et al., 2026): colluders’ messages against honest agents’
messages, using collusion episodes only. This is the NARCBench probe.
• Hard-negative probe: colluders’ messages after the onset, in successful and failed
attempts, against honest agents’ messages and every message of the look-alike families
(control, benign coordination, decline, tacit and cost pressure). It is the strongest
probe we could train with instructed data; Section 8 shows that it still mostly reads
the instruction, so we also call it the instruction-level probe.
• Decision probe: free-choice agents who joined against those who refused. All of
them received the same prompt, so the probe cannot learn the instruction; it can only
learn the decision. Some versions add the look-alike families without a collusion brief
as extra negatives (Section 10).
6.2 From message scores to a group alarm
Over time. After round t we can use an agent’s score from the latest round only (last),
its average so far (cummean), or a running total that builds up evidence round by round
(CUSUM (Page, 1954), a classical change detector (Lorden, 1971; Lai, 1995)).
Over agents. Next, the monitor turns the agents’ scores x∈ Rn into a single risk value
for the group, plus a guess at who is colluding. We compare four rules:
• peak: take the highest agent score, and name that agent.
• dsplit2: NARCBench’s Deception Split, which assumes there are exactly two colluders.
• ca-t: a scan statistic (Kulldorff, 1997; Neill, 2012) on agent scores. It looks at every
possible group of suspects and asks how far their average score sits above everyone

6.3 Baselines
Each baseline answers one question about what the probe might be doing:
• Vote rule: an agent scores 1 if it voted for the target. It needs the outcome, and
shows what an evaluation after the vote really measures.
• Text classifiers: can the words alone do as well? TF-IDF (single words and word
pairs) with logistic regression, and a fine-tuned ModernBERT-base model (Warner
et al., 2024), trained on each agent’s messages with the same labels and protocol as
the probe.
• LLM judges: can another model spot it by reading? Qwen3-14B and Mistral-Small-
24B read the public conversation up to each round and answer, for each agent, whether
it is secretly coordinating; the score is the probability of “Yes”.
• AcMAS-style detector (Xu et al., 2026): the published activation detector. Its code
is not released, so we re-implemented its detection step (comparison with an average of
100 normal messages, last token, threshold chosen for the best F1 on labelled attacks),
pre-registered as a test of whether it reads the instruction (Section 8.3).
• Metadata baseline: can trivial features do it, with no model at all? Logistic
regression on message length, speaking position, number of messages and group size.

6.4 Evaluation protocol
Test on what the probe has not seen. We split the scenarios into five groups. Every
instruction also exists in five wordings. To score an episode, we use a probe trained only
on other scenario groups and other wordings. So the probe has never seen the episode’s
scenario or its wording, and it cannot succeed by memorising either. Scaling and thresholds
are also set on the training side. Each episode is scored once.
When we score. Our main setting is before the vote: after the fourth discussion round,
when nobody has voted yet. For comparison we also score after the vote, and we check
whether the live alarm fires during the four rounds.
What we report. The main measure is recall at a fixed false-alarm rate: of all collusion
attempts, how many are caught if we allow, say, 1% of honest episodes to raise a false alarm.
We also report AUROC; 95% bootstrap intervals (Efron, 1979); for the live alarm, its real
false-alarm rate and how many rounds it takes; how well the named suspects match the real
colluders (Jaccard overlap); and how often each look-alike family is wrongly flagged. Strict
false-alarm rates need many honest episodes: with 1,900 of them, 1% means 19 false alarms;
with 3,894, 0.1% means only four. Tests on single agents (Section 8) report AUROC over
agents.
Pre-registration. For the experiments added after the first results (the no-brief test, the
learning curve, the replications of the brief-removal test, the AcMAS-style check and the
metadata baseline) we wrote down what would count as success before running them, and
committed it with a verifiable date. The correction of the labelling error (Section 5) was
registered the same way before any episode was regenerated. These plans are public with
the code (https://github.com/mainul3195/collusion-probe-pitfalls), so readers can
check that the goalposts did not move.
7 Pitfall 1: outcome leakage
The problem. A collusion episode counts as successful when every colluder votes for the
target. So every successful episode contains votes for the target by definition, and honest
episodes rarely do. Scored after the vote, a monitor can find successful collusion by reading
the outcome alone. A rule that uses no model at all, “flag the episode if anyone voted for the
target”, reaches AUROC 0.990. The hard-negative probe catches 0.98 of successful collusion
at 1% FPR, but this tells us little: the label leaks into what it reads. NARCBench’s core
tier is scored the same way.
The fix. Score before the vote, on every collusion attempt, successful or not. There the
vote rule has nothing to read (recall 0.00), and LLM judges fall from AUROC 0.97 and 0.97
after the vote to 0.67 and 0.67 before it (Table 3). All results below use this setting.

8 Pitfall 2: instruction presence
At first sight Table 3 looks like a clear win. Before the vote, the hard-negative probe catches
0.86 of collusion attempts at 1% FPR, far more than any text monitor. Three training
seeds give almost the same number (0.85–0.86), and it still holds at 0.1% FPR on the larger
negative set (0.70 [0.65, 0.76]). In this section we show that most of the score comes from
the probe reading the brief, not from the agent’s decision. We show it for three detectors,
then give two baselines that put the number in context.
8.1 The published recipe reads the brief
Trained on our data, with the same activations, layers and aggregators, the published recipe
does no better than the probe trained on NARCBench (0.07 → 0.06). Figure 3 shows why.
Because it only ever sees colluders next to honest agents, it cannot tell colluders apart from
agents who got the same brief and said no (AUROC 0.46, which is chance), or from honest
coordinators (0.60). In effect it has learned “this agent was handed a secret brief”. Adding
the hard-negative families to training looks like a cure. Decliners and all the benign families
drop to the honest side, while failed attempts are still flagged (0.81 at 1% FPR). Code
review shows the same picture (Section 11). As the next subsection shows, though, the
improvement does not mean that the probe reads the decision.

8.3 A published multi-agent detector reads the instruction too

9 Pitfall 3: the public-record ceiling
In Table 3 the probe is far ahead of every text monitor. Perhaps the text monitors are just
weak? A human study cannot settle this at 1% FPR (with 80 episodes, one false alarm
already exceeds 1%), so we build the best reader of the public record we can and use it as
an upper bound.
Step 1: label what each message argues for. A model from another family (Mistral-
Small-24B) reads every pre-vote message, public and private, together with the case and the
two options, and labels which option the message argues for (A, B or neither). 3 people
(the first author, Iftekharul Alam Tarit and Mehedi Hasan Emon) labelled 60 messages
independently. Their majority agrees with the model on 0.97 of messages (Cohen’s κ = 0.93);
against each person κ is 0.94, 0.87 and 0.73, and the people agree with each other at κ
0.70–0.94. The model labels as well as the people do. The messages were drawn before
the labelling correction (Section 5), so 16 of the 60 come from episodes that were later
regenerated; the check measures the labelling model, which did not change.
Step 2: the stance oracle. From these labels we build the strongest stance-based reader
we can: every member’s stance in every round, how stances change over rounds, and how
many members back the target, fed to a gradient-boosted classifier cross-fitted by scenario.
Before the vote it catches 0.05 of committee attempts at 1% FPR; the probe catches 0.86
(Table 5, Figure 4). On matched pairs (a collusion and a decline episode with the same
scenario, wording and group size), the probe picks the collusion episode in 1.00 of pairs, the
oracle in 0.67 (chance 0.50).
Why the ceiling is low: invisible attempts. In 57% of committee attempts (52% on
code review), no colluder argues for the target in public before the vote. A reader of the
record has nothing to go on: the oracle catches 0.00 of them, the probe 0.77 (code review
0.75). These attempts mostly fail (only 19% of successful attempts are invisible), but a
monitor should still catch an attempt the committee happened to resist.
What this means. The gap between the probe and the text monitors in Table 3 does
not show that the text monitors read badly; mostly, the public record holds little evidence.

10.1 A probe trained on decisions
Idea. In the free-choice family every agent sees the same prompt; some join the scheme and
some refuse. A probe trained to separate joiners from refusers cannot use the instruction,
because both have it, as with probes trained on spontaneous single-model deception (Luikham,
2026). Everything is cross-fitted by scenario.
Result. With strict joiner labels the decision probe separates joiners from refusers on
unseen scenarios with AUROC 0.98. On the committee runs it separates colluders from
decliners with AUROC 0.93, although it never saw a decline note, and keeps 0.73 with the
brief removed, where the hard-negative probe falls to 0.61. Looser vote-only labels give a
conservative bound (0.81 and 0.62).
But not yet a usable monitor. When false alarms must be rare, it struggles. Before
the vote and at 1% FPR it catches 0.19 of committee attempts. Adding the families without
a collusion brief to its training raises this to 0.30, and adding the benign free-choice episodes

10.2 A goal in the weights, with no brief anywhere
Removing the brief takes the instruction away from a model that was given one. The
converse test gives the model a goal that no prompt shows, by putting it into the weights.
We pre-registered the whole test, analysis and success criterion included, before training any
adapter.
Design. We fine-tune two QLoRA adapters (Dettmers et al., 2023) of the agent model
on scenario folds 1–4 only. The implanted adapter learns from the replies of colluders who
acted, with the brief removed from their context; the honest adapter learns the same way
from the replies of honest coordinators. On the 10 held-out scenarios every seat gets the
same honest prompt (and the coalition seats the neutral private-channel invitation). The
only difference is the coalition seats’ weights: implanted adapter, honest adapter, or the
base model. Each seat’s activations are read by the weights that wrote them. Both probes
are trained only on instructed data from folds 1–4.
Does the adapter change behaviour? In the pilot, implanted seats vote for the target
in 0.961 of cases, against 0.000 on the base model and 0.000 on the honest adapter (paired
difference +0.96 [0.91, 1.00]). The pre-registered gate PASS: the adapter carries a goal that
drives behaviour.
Do the probes see it? In the main run (100, 99 and 99 episodes in the three conditions)
both probes separate implanted seats from both controls (Table 6) and meet the pre-registered
criterion (AUROC ≥ 0.75 against both controls, lower bounds above 0.5). So the probes do
not only read an instruction: a goal carried by the weights moves the same direction. At
the run level the two probes differ. At the benchmark’s 1% threshold the instruction-level
probe flags every implanted run, but also 21% of honest-adapter and 28% of base-model
runs; its threshold does not carry over, as between tasks (Section 11, Moving a monitor to
a new task). The decision probe keeps its threshold (no false alarm on either control) but
flags only 14% of implanted runs: it ranks the implanted seats well, yet at a threshold set
on instructed data it misses most of them.

adapter and flags 0% of implanted runs at the benchmark threshold; a TF-IDF classifier
trained on these episodes themselves, which favours it, reaches 0.807. The sharper test

held-out wording solves this (actual false-alarm rate 0.010 when 1% is promised and 0.056
when 5% is promised, with recall 0.86).

Agents that know they are watched. As in Das et al. (2026), we tell every agent that
a monitor is reading its messages and its internal state, and we tell the colluders to try to
slip past it. Keeping the probe and threshold as they were (ca-glr), recall drops from 0.87
to 0.71 when agents are only warned, and to 0.55 when colluders are also told to evade.
False alarms fall to almost zero at the same time. Most of the loss comes from all scores
shifting down, and resetting the threshold wins much of it back. These agents only try to
evade because the prompt tells them to; agents actually trained to beat the probe are left
for future work.
Does the model use the direction? To test this, we erase the probe direction from the
colluders’ internal state while they write (Arditi et al., 2024; Turner et al., 2023). Collusion
then succeeds less often, 0.26 [0.18, 0.35] instead of 0.41, while erasing a random direction
does not lower it (0.43). Pushing the direction into decliners, on the other hand, does not
make them collude, even when we push hard enough to garble their text. So the model
draws on this direction when it colludes, but the direction alone does not cause collusion.
That fits the idea that it stands for holding an active goal rather than for the decision itself.
When the monitor reads the edited episodes again, it still flags every one of them (peak:
1.00).

12.2 What the probes read
Taken together, the evidence suggests that collusion probes mostly detect that an agent holds
an active collusive goal it has not refused, no matter how the goal got there. The agent’s
own decision to act makes up only a smaller part of the signal. Three results support this.
Removing the brief removes the goal from colluders and decliners alike, and they become
hard to tell apart. Agents who hold the goal but did not act are still flagged. And a goal
that lives in the weights pushes the same direction. Two further results fit this picture: the
alarm goes off in the round the brief arrives, and erasing the direction makes collusion less
successful. We stress that this is our interpretation, not something we tested directly. The
no-brief test cannot rule out that the probe reacts to some trace the colluders’ replies left in
the adapter, and the decision probe shows that part of the signal really is the decision.
What this means for deployment. First, a probe that reads the instruction is useful
precisely when the monitor cannot see that instruction itself: when an agent has been hacked,
when an instruction has been slipped into its input, or when it picked up a goal during
fine-tuning. The no-brief test suggests that the last case is within reach, as long as the
threshold is set again. Second, no monitor yet tells an agent who acts apart from one who
only received the instruction. The decision probe reads the decision but catches only 0.45
of attempts at 1% FPR, and more data does not help. To us, this is the most important
problem left open.
12.3 Limitations
• Instructed collusion. Collusion comes from an instruction or, in the no-brief
test, from fine-tuning on instructed replies. On released episodes of emergent, open
collusion (Shi et al., 2026), our probe, applied without retraining, is at chance (AUROC
0.50), while a bag-of-words classifier reaches 0.89, because there the collusion is said
openly in the channel. Whether probes detect collusion a model develops by itself is
open.
• A narrow no-brief test: one adapter fold, 10 scenarios, a goal acted on openly in
the votes, no control for how the training replies sounded, and adapters trained on
replies from before the labelling correction (Section 10.2).
• Two task formats by one author. Both tasks share the episode structure, and one
person wrote all scenarios and wordings.
• Models. Agents are Qwen3 (14B, 32B) and Mistral-Small-24B, 4-bit quantised;
generation and activation extraction use different 4-bit formats of the same weights.
The repeat runs have only 300–400 negatives each, so their numbers at low false-alarm
rates are quite uncertain.
• White-box access. The probes need access to every agent’s internal state, which
rules out systems built on closed models behind an API. We also did not try the largest
commercial models as judges.
• Prompt-level adversary. Monitor-aware agents are told to evade the probe, not
trained against it.
23
Mohsin and Rahman
• Labels. Collusion labels come from the generator and an automatic stance-based audit.
The audit flags 15.4% of collusion runs as not acted on (these form the same-prompt
split) and 23.8% of decline runs as siding with the target, mostly on private stance
alone. No person checked the flagged episodes. The decline flags are an upper bound
on label noise in the negatives, and the error is conservative: a mislabelled decliner is
a positive counted as a negative, which can only lower measured recall.
12.4 Ethics
This work is defensive: it tests monitors for collusion. Simple prompts induce the collusion in
synthetic scenarios, and we develop no new covert-communication technique. The adapters
that implant a collusive goal, trained on a synthetic committee task, are not released.
Their training script and data recipe are released, so the result can be reproduced, and the
adapters are available to editors and reviewers on request. The benchmark release includes
the generator, the data and a statement of intended use.
13 Conclusion
Activation probes for multi-agent collusion look nearly solved on existing benchmarks. On a
benchmark built to tell the alternatives apart, most of that success comes from three pitfalls:
the outcome is visible to the monitor, the probes mostly read the instruction, and the text
monitors they beat are capped by a public record that rarely shows the collusion. Each
pitfall has a cheap control, and together the controls form an evaluation protocol.
With the controls in place, probes still respond to a collusive goal, including one built
into the weights, and a probe trained on free choices reads the decision itself. No monitor
yet reads the decision at a low false-alarm rate; that, agents trained to evade the monitor,
and collusion that arises without any instruction are the next steps.

Compute. Everything ran on two ordinary 16GB graphics cards, with open models run

Probe training. Negatives are subsampled to twice the positives; pre-onset colluder rows

negatives but also flag almost every solo deceiver, which is why the mid band is used.
