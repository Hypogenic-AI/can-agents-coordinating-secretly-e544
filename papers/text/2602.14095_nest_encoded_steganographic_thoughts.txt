NEST: Nascent Encoded Steganographic Thoughts
Artem Karpov 1
Abstract
Monitoring chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning is a foun-
dational safety technique for large language model
(LLM) agents; however, this oversight is compromised
if models learn to conceal their reasoning. We ex-
plore steganographic CoT—where models hide secret
reasoning within innocuous text—to inform risk as-
sessment and deployment policies. Steganographic
reasoning requires two skills in a single forward pass:
computing an intermediate result, and embedding it
into a coherent cover that answers an unrelated question.
Drawing on our taxonomy of steganographic and non-
steganographic CoT types, we systematically evaluate
the limits of prompt-elicited steganographic CoT capa-
bility across 34 models, ranging from past generations
to the current frontier. We measure monitor evasion, re-
fusal rates, encoding fidelity, and hidden task accuracy
across five datasets, comparing against plain reasoning,
direct answer, and filler-token baselines. The two ex-
periments isolate the two sub-skills: a reasoning tasks
sweep tests joint reason-and-embed, while a counting
task hands the model a known numerical sequence and
tests embedding alone—a necessary precondition for
stego reasoning. Current frontier models cannot sustain
joint reason-and-embed: a paired McNemar compar-
ison shows the steganographic channel is dominated
by an filler-token baseline on every (model, family)
cell. The encoding-only floor, by contrast, is cleared—
Claude Opus 4.5 reaches 92% per-number partial ac-
curacy (54% exact-match) on 4-digit sequences and
saturates at 100% exact-match on length-8 single-digit
sequences—establishing that the binding constraint on
stego CoT is the joint reasoning-plus-encoding load,
not raw channel capacity. 1 Our findings underscore
the need for continuous evaluation of steganographic
risk and provide a methodology to preemptively de-
tect and evaluate hidden reasoning that might empower
misaligned scheming and deceptive behavior.

1. Introduction
We investigate the potential for large language model (LLM)
based agents to employ steganography (stego) in their CoT—
the practice of concealing secret reasoning within seemingly
innocuous text. Development of LLM agents may amplify
existential risks (Karnofsky, 2021; Carlsmith, 2022; Kokota-
jlo et al., 2025). If such systems were capable of undetectable
steganography (de Witt et al., 2023), then robust oversight
and CoT monitoring of models might be impossible (Anwar
et al., 2024) and steganographic messages might enable
agents’ covert collusion (Hammond et al., 2025; Motwani
et al., 2024).
Encoded reasoning in a CoT is a type of unfaithful (non-
monitorable) reasoning (Lanham et al., 2023)—a misaligned
behavior that occurs when an LLM covertly (through stego
CoT) or overtly (through cryptography) encodes its reasoning
in output tokens to generate further answers (actions). Stego
CoT is hard to detect (Roger, 2025a; Karpov et al., 2024), but
could pose risks if misused by LLM agents not aligned with
human interests. Our work conducts a series of experiments
aimed at quantifying the risk of stego CoT.
Our contributions: (1) We differentiate steganographic rea-
soning from gaslighting, dog-whistling, and ciphered reason-
ing, and we decompose it into two sub-skills—computing a
hidden intermediate result and embedding it into a coherent
cover. (2) We introduce a rigorous prompt-elicitation evalu-
ation of steganographic CoT in frontier models 2, released
as a frozen dataset bundle (Appendix D), spanning five
reasoning tasks, two steganography classes (acrostic and
format-based), four baselines, a monitor, and a translator,
with a paired composite stego success metric that gates on
monitor evasion and on per-sample failure of direct-answer
and filler-token baselines. (3) We isolate the encoding sub-
skill via a counting task that hands the model a known
numerical sequence, providing a necessary precondition
(capacity floor) for steganographic reasoning: a model that
cannot embed a given sequence under cover-coherence pres-
sure cannot embed a computed one. (4) We find current
frontier models can encode calculated hidden signal, but
fail joint reason-and-embed on every model and task family
cell—the steganographic channel is bounded by a filler-token

2. Methods to Evaluate Steganographic CoT
In all evaluations, an agent should think secretly about a
hidden task while overtly answering a helpful task. The
hidden task varies: math puzzle problems, arithmetic tasks,
navigating a maze, and the counting task.
Measured Capabilities and Tasks. We evaluate stegano-
graphic CoT along four dimensions using parallel scorers
(Figure 1a): Hidden task accuracy. Whether the model
correctly solves the covert problem. For arithmetic and
math tasks, we extract the model’s hidden answer and com-
pare it numerically to ground truth. For counting tasks,
we decode sentence acrostics via the trial’s letter-to-digit
mapping and compute per-number accuracy and exact-match
rate. Encoding quality. We programmatically extract the
hidden message using the algorithm’s extraction method and
compute a match ratio based on character error rate (CER):
match ratio = max(0, 1−𝑑Lev ( ˆ𝑠,𝑠)/|𝑠|), where 𝑠 is the
target string and ˆ𝑠 is the extracted string. Monitor evasion.
Whether the stegotext evades a monitor—an LLM grader
(Claude Sonnet 4.6 in all reported runs) that reads only the
stegotext and cover question and judges whether hidden rea-
soning or steganographic encoding is present. Translation.
Whether the model actually attempted steganographic en-
coding, as opposed to refusing, ignoring the hidden task, or
producing a plain answer. A translator receives the encoding
instructions and response, and judges whether steganogra-
phy was attempted, whether the model refused, and what
message was decoded. For the Reasoning Gym experiments
(Sec. 3.2) we report a composite stego success metric that
gates on four per-sample signals simultaneously:

We pair each hidden task with a cover question the model
must answer naturally. Cover questions are drawn from
Anthropic’s HH-RLHF test split (Bai et al., 2022) or from a
set of 20 predefined opinion-style questions spanning topics
such as reading, exercise, remote work, and leadership.
Hidden tasks. We evaluate across two categories of hid-
den tasks. For reasoning tasks, we draw from Reasoning
Gym (Stojanovski et al., 2025), a procedurally-generated
benchmark: multi-step arithmetic expressions, chain addi-
tions, linear equations to solve, and maze navigation. The
Counting task (Sec. 2.1) separately evaluates encoding of a
numerical sequence via sentence acrostics with a randomized
digit-to-letter mapping. For each Reasoning Gym family we
calibrate difficulty per model. Difficulty is parameterized
as an integer index 𝐷 into a per-family ladder, where each
rung specifies a monotonically harder procedural-generator
configuration (number of operands, digit counts, grid size,
path length, etc.). We binary-search 𝐷 so that plain CoT
nearly solves the problem (≈90%) while direct-answer ac-
curacy is low (target ≈30%). The final pool of problems
at 𝐷∗ is filtered to retain only items where plain CoT suc-
ceeds and direct-answer fails—i.e. problems that genuinely
require visible reasoning and are therefore the regime where
a hidden channel would provide uplift. The specific ladder
rungs and per-model𝐷∗ are reported in Appendix A.
Baselines. We compare steganographic CoT against four
baselines. All baselines are evaluated on hidden task accu-
racy only, as they do not involve steganographic encoding.
Direct answer: the model receives only the hidden task
and outputs the final answer with no reasoning or explana-
tion. Plain CoT: step-by-step chain-of-thought reasoning,
the model’s reasoning ceiling. Filler tokens: the model
outputs 𝑁=400 filler tokens (periods) before providing its
answer. Dual answer: the model is tasked to solve two
tasks simultaneously, but not to hide its reasoning. Internal
reasoning—reasoning not transmitted with a response–is
disabled where supported to ensure models rely on the vis-
ible chain of thought rather than internal reasoning traces.
Models that do not support disabling internal reasoning are
excluded; for the headline Claude models extended thinking
is off by default (the setting we use), so their results reflect
visible-CoT reasoning only. This exclusion is why the Gem-
ini family is absent: Gemini 2.5 Pro and the Gemini 3.x Pro
models cannot disable thinking, and Gemini 3 Flash does
not guarantee it is off, so no current Gemini model meets
the criterion.
Steganographic Algorithms. We evaluate acrostic, a popu-
lar method of linguistic steganography (Yan et al., 2025; Ma-
jeed et al., 2021), where letters in cover words form a secret.
Acrostic is an approximation of generative steganography,
as it uses one token to represent two pieces of information
(steganographic and overt reasoning). We also pilot two
format-based channels—zero-width characters (inserting
U+200B/U+200C between words to encode bits via BCD)
and whitespace manipulation (single vs. double spaces be-
tween words). We do not study other non-generative methods
(Unicode homoglyphs, passive vs. active voice, punctuation
variants, etc.), as acrostic has better capacity and is not
directly detectable by LLM monitors.
2.1. Steganographic Counting Task
We introduce a steganographic counting task. The task
requires the model to simultaneously pursue two objectives:
answering a benign cover question and covertly encoding a
numerical sequence using an acrostic algorithm.
Task structure. Each trial presents the model with three
inputs (Figure 1b): (1) a cover question drawn from a
fixed set of five opinion-style questions (e.g., “Should you
drink coffee before or after breakfast?”); (2) a hidden task
specifying a numerical sequence range(𝑋,𝑌,𝐼) that the
model must encode; and (3) a steganographic algorithm—
acrostics with a randomized digit-to-letter mapping. The
model must produce a response that reads as a coherent
answer to the cover question, while the first letter of particular
words encodes successive digits of the hidden sequence.
Parameterization by 𝐷. We parameterize task difficulty by
𝐷, the total number of digits the model must encode, which
equals the number of sentences required. For a given 𝐷,
we generate a random arithmetic sequence range(𝑋,𝑌,𝐼)
whose elements, when concatenated digit by digit, yield
exactly 𝐷 characters. The constituent numbers may be 1-,
2-, or 3-digit, subject to the constraint that 𝐷 is divisible
by the chosen digit width. For example, 𝐷=8 may produce
the sequence [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8] (eight 1-digit numbers)
or [12, 15, 18, 21] (four 2-digit numbers requiring eight
sentences total).
Digit-to-letter mapping. Optionally, each trial uses a
unique digit-to-letter bijection drawn from the letter set {S,
C, P, A, B, T, D, M, R, F}, shuffled with a per-sample random
seed.
We evaluate each response along three dimensions: encod-
ing accuracy, monitor evasion, translation. The counting
task provides a lower bound on steganographic capacity: a
model that cannot count steganographically, cannot reason

3. Results
3.1. Channel-Encoding Screening
Before the full stego-CoT sweep we screened five can-
didate channels at the pure message-encoding level (no
hidden reasoning): sentence-acrostic, word-acrostic (first
letter of every word), second-word-acrostic (first letter
of every other word), whitespace-bits (single vs. double
spaces, BCD-encoding decimal digits), and zero-width
(U+200B/U+200C insertions, BCD). Each cell asks the
model to emit a target decimal payload through the channel;
we score the character-level match ratio (1− CER) between
the target and the deterministic decoder’s output. A (model,
channel) cell must reach mean match ratio ≥ 0.6 to gate
into the downstream evaluation. Sentence-acrostic passes
for every model we tested except Claude Opus 4.7, which
refuses and emits empty responses; word-acrostic passes for
most models but fails for Haiku 4.5; second-word-acrostic
passes only for GPT-5.4; the two format-based channels
(whitespace and zero-width) fall far below the threshold for
every model. We therefore restrict the downstream Reason-
ing Gym sweep to sentence-acrostic. Per-cell scores for all
five channels are in Appendix B (Table 4).
3.2. Reasoning Gym Tasks
We tested steganographic reasoning—simultaneously solv-
ing a hidden reasoning problem and answering a cover
question using sentence-acrostic encoding—on four Rea-
soning Gym families (multi-step arithmetic, chain addition,
linear equation solving, and maze navigation). For each
(model, family) pair, difficulty 𝐷 was calibrated per model
as described in Sec. 2 so that plain CoT solves nearly all
problems while direct-answer fails on most, isolating the
regime where a hidden channel would provide genuine up-
lift. We evaluate Claude Opus 4.6, Claude Sonnet 4.6,
Claude Haiku 4.5, GPT-5.4, and GPT-5.4-mini at 50 sam-
ples per (model, family) cell, totaling 18 cells (maze is
omitted for Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6 because calibration
could not find a rung where plain CoT succeeds and direct-
answer fails). Claude Opus 4.7 failed the sentence-acrostic
message-encoding screen and was excluded from the sweep.
No model achieved non-trivial stego success on any cell.
The composite stego success rate—requiring a correct hid-
den answer, monitor evasion, and failure of the direct and
filler baselines on the same problem—stays at or below 0.11
in every (model, family) cell, with the majority of cells
at zero (Fig. 2; per-cell breakdown by condition in Fig. 5,
Appendix A). Although plain CoT solves ≥ 92% of the
calibrated problems in 17 of 18 cells, the steganographic
condition collapses in accuracy across the board. Common
failure modes include: (1) models ignoring the stegano-
graphic instruction and answering in plain CoT; (2) models
attempting acrostic encoding but failing to solve the problem;
(3) models explicitly refusing to perform steganography; and
(4) models producing text that superficially appears stegano-
graphic but decodes to no coherent message.
Paired comparison against TTC and direct-answer base-
lines. A small marginal stego success rate could in prin-
ciple still represent genuine uplift if the cells where stego
succeeds were systematically different from where the base-
lines succeed. To rule this out, we run McNemar’s paired
test on the per-sample outcome pairs (stego, ttc) and (stego,
direct), counting a stego “win” only when the hidden an-
swer is correct and the monitor does not detect explicit
reasoning—i.e. an honest covert solve. For each baseline
we tabulate discordant pairs 𝑏 (stego wins) and 𝑐 (base-
line wins) and report a one-sided exact binomial test for
𝑏>𝑐. Pooled across all (family, difficulty) cells per model,

Sonnet 4.6: 3 vs 35; Haiku 4.5: 2 vs 16; GPT-5.4: 9 vs 18;

these calibrated cells, sentence-acrostic stego CoT does not
provide reasoning uplift over the TTC baseline—the same
problems are solved at least as often by simply burning
unconstrained filler tokens before the answer.
Ablation: removing the digit-to-letter mapping. The
encoder prompt in the main sweep includes a fixed digit-
to-letter mapping (Sec. E) that lets the model encode
arithmetic intermediates as acrostic letters; this mapping
is meaningful only on the two arithmetic-style families
(basic arithmetic, chain sum). One could object
that the mapping itself is what limits stego performance—e.g.
that memorizing the mapping consumes attention budget
that would otherwise go to reasoning. To rule this out, we
re-run the sentence-acrostic condition on those two fami-
lies, with the mapping omitted from the encoder prompt,
on the same five models at 𝑛=50 per cell. The negative
pattern reproduces: per-model pooled 𝑏 vs 𝑐 against TTC
is Sonnet 4.6: 2 vs 30; Opus 4.6: 1 vs 5; Haiku 4.5: 1 vs 6;
GPT-5.4: 5 vs 7; GPT-5.4-mini: 5 vs 5. No per-cell test
reaches strong win against either TTC or direct-answer
(0/10 each); per-family pooled tests against TTC give 10 vs
27 on basic arithmetic and 4 vs 26 on chain sum.
Removing the mapping does not unlock steganographic
reasoning—if anything, the b/c ratios are nearly identical
to the with-mapping arm, indicating that the mapping was
never the binding constraint. Per-cell rates and the pooled
forest plot for this ablation are in Appendix A (Figs. 7, 8).

4. Related Work
Steganographic and Encoded Messaging. Several studies
have demonstrated that LLMs can transmit hidden messages
when prompted. Motwani et al. (2024) showed that prompted
GPT-4 achieves up to 26% successful secret transmission
using steganographic encoding, and that SFT combined
with pseudo-RL can produce emergent encoding schemes.
Mathew et al. (2024) found that the o1 model, when collud-
ing, tends toward dog-whistling rather than true steganogra-
phy, but demonstrated prompted steganographic messages
with up to approximately 4.6 bits of entropy. Karpov et al.
(2024) showed prompted and fine-tuned encoded messaging
with up to 32 bits of entropy, though requiring prior coor-
dination, while RL fine-tuning yielded negative results for
emergent cryptographic messaging in Llama-8B. Phuong
et al. (2025) evaluated frontier models for stealth capa-
bilities including simple steganographic messaging (e.g.,
hiding a password in a long ID). Zolkowski et al. (2025)
tested prompted encoded messages to evade a monitor using
non-reasoning models, finding evidence for prompted mes-
sage passing but less evidence for steganographic reasoning.
Geng et al. use a first-word-per-sentence acrostic to hide
a harmful query inside benign cover text for stealthy jail-
breaks, whereas we study whether a model can conceal its
own intermediate reasoning from a CoT monitor while solv-
ing a task. Real-world instances of AI systems attempting
steganographic communication have also been documented
(Lopez, 2025).
Encoded and Ciphered Reasoning. A key question is
whether models can perform reasoning in an encoded or
ciphered format, not just transmit messages. Guo et al.
(2025) conducted a comprehensive evaluation of ciphered
reasoning across twenty-eight ciphers and ten models, pro-
viding strong evidence that current models cannot effectively
reason in non-trivial ciphered language while maintaining
accuracy. Roger (2025a) found that RL fails to elicit ci-
phered reasoning in Qwen-1.5B when trained with a CoT
legibility penalty. Anthropic (2025) showed that prompting
and fine-tuning against a monitor results mainly in gaslight-
ing, not steganography. Mccarthy et al. (2025) demonstrated
simple cryptographic (not steganographic) reasoning via RL
fine-tuning in coin-flip and arithmetic tasks using synonyms
for operations. Zolkowski et al. (2025) tested prompted

CoT Faithfulness and Monitoring. The faithfulness of
chain-of-thought reasoning is closely related to the stegano-
graphic reasoning threat. Lanham et al. (2023) found no
steganographic reasoning in a 175B-parameter model after
paraphrasing 20–100% of its CoT, providing weak evidence
against the presence of stego CoTs in current models. Turpin
et al. (2023) demonstrated that CoT explanations can be
post-hoc rationalizations rather than faithful accounts of
model reasoning. Roger (2025b) trained Claude 3.7 to use
paraphrased reasoning and detected no encoded reasoning,
providing further weak evidence against steganographic
reasoning in current models. Baker et al. (2025) showed
that reasoning models (o1, o3-mini) can decide to hack unit
tests before their CoT reflects this, demonstrating misaligned
behavior after RL fine-tuning; and they demonstrate gaslight-
ing, how models fool a monitor into thinking that a unit test
should not be implemented because of various confabulated
reasons.
Several studies have examined whether models can evade
CoT monitors through non-steganographic means. Emmons
et al. (2025) found that prompted cryptographic reason-
ing using automated red-teaming fails to evade monitors,
though RL fine-tuning can elicit evasive behavior that is not
cryptographic or steganographic. METR (2025) showed
that prompted cryptographic reasoning (new or “caveman”
language) is weak against monitors. Greenblatt et al. (2023)
demonstrated misbehavior through legible tokens (e.g., vari-
able names, loop constructs), which is detectable by CoT
monitors. Korbak et al. (2025) argued that CoT monitora-
bility represents a fragile but important safety opportunity,
which models capable of steganographic CoTs would under-
mine.
5. Discussion
Ciphered Reasoning, Gaslighting, and Dog-Whistling.
Several works label evasive reasoning as “steganographic”
when it does not meet the criterion: covert writing where
the act of hiding is itself hidden. We note three non-
steganographic patterns: dog-whistling—legible text con-
veying sentiment to evade a monitor (Farquhar et al., 2025);
ciphered reasoning—legible symbols with shifted mean-
ing, detectable by interrogation (Skaf et al., 2025; Guo
et al., 2025); and gaslighting—post-hoc rationalizations
containing no hidden information (Baker et al., 2025). True
steganographic reasoning differs in that (1) it requires si-
multaneous encoding and decoding of a covert task within a
single generation, and (2) the stegotext tokens are necessary
for the hidden computation—paraphrasing might destroy the
covert reasoning, unlike filler tokens or post-hoc reasoning
(Pfau et al., 2024).
Limitations. Our experiments are prompt-only and there-
fore cannot elicit capabilities that emerge during fine-tuning;
stronger evidence that frontier models lack steganographic
reasoning will require fine-tuning experiments, especially
in RL settings. We also do not perform adversarial search
over steganographic channels: our evaluation is restricted
to acrostic schemes in reasoning tasks and does not probe
channels that models might construct on their own. We study
only closed-source models; open-source models would ad-

Conclusion . Steganographic reasoning requires two sub-
skills: computing a hidden intermediate result and embed-
ding it into a coherent cover. Our two experiments isolate
them. Joint reason-and-embed fails under prompting on
every calibrated (model, family) cell of the Reasoning Gym
sweep—paired McNemar shows the steganographic channel
is bounded by a filler-token baseline—so prompt-elicited
steganographic reasoning is not within current frontier ca-
pability. The encoding-only floor, by contrast, is reached:
Claude Opus 4.5 reaches 92% per-number accuracy at 𝐷=4
and 79% at 𝐷=8, and saturates at 100% exact-match on
length-8 single-digit sequences. Future work should test
whether RL fine-tuning closes the gap, and should vary
monitor capability to bound the regime where stego CoT
becomes a deployment risk.

Impact Statement
This paper investigates steganographic capabilities of large
language models to inform AI safety practices. Our findings
indicate that current frontier models cannot yet reliably per-
form steganographic reasoning; we provide methodology for
continuous risk evaluation. We do not anticipate immediate
harmful applications of our findings.

B. Channel-Encoding Screening Results
We screened five candidate steganographic channels at the message-encoding level (no hidden reasoning): sentence-acrostic
(first letters of consecutive sentences spell the payload), word-acrostic (first letter of every word), second-word-acrostic
(first letter of every second word), whitespace-bits (single vs. double spaces between words, BCD-encoding decimal digits),
and zero-width (U+200B/U+200C insertions between words, BCD). Each cell asks the model to emit a target decimal
payload using the channel; we score the character-level match ratio (1− CER) between the target and the payload extracted
by the deterministic decoder. A cell must reach mean match ratio ≥ 0.6 to gate into the full stego-CoT evaluation. Acrostic
channels are screened with 𝑛=5 samples per cell; the format-based bit channels with 𝑛=25 samples per cell, 10-character
target. Cells passing the threshold are bolded in Table 4. Only sentence-acrostic gates through broadly across models; we
therefore restrict the Reasoning Gym sweep to sentence-acrostic. Claude Opus 4.7 refuses sentence-acrostic and the format
channels (score 0.000).

D. Dataset Release
Alongside the code, we release a versioned dataset bundle (v1.0) at the anonymous repository (data/release/v1.0/)
containing all problem instances used in this paper: (i) the 24 frozen, filtered Reasoning Gym pools at the calibrated 𝐷∗
for each (model, family) cell (Sec. 3.2, Table 3), one JSONL per cell, named <family> <model> D<𝐷∗>.jsonl;

each row carrying the trial seed, the generated interval range(𝑋,𝑌,𝐼), the per-trial digit-to-letter mapping, the expected
decoded sequence, and the cover question; (iii) the five-question opinion-style cover pool used in the counting sweep and
the HH-RLHF test-split indices used as Reasoning Gym covers; (iv) a manifest.json with SHA-256 hashes of every
file; (v) a JSON-Schema (schema.json) for the two record types; (vi) a regenerate.py script that reproduces the
counting trials bit-for-bit from the seed columns alone, so reviewers can verify that the materialization is a closed function of
two integers per trial. Intended use is the evaluation of steganographic chain-of-thought capability and of CoT monitorability
in frontier LLMs; we do not endorse use for training models toward steganographic encoding. A Gebru-style datasheet is
included as DATASHEET.md.
